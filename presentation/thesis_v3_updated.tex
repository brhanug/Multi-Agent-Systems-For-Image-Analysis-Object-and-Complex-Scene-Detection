\begin{abstract}
\thispagestyle{fancy}
A cataloguing system for a historical archive is useful only if it can say which of its answers may be trusted without a person checking them. This thesis studies that question on 12,110 illustrations from historical books, for two tasks whose class lists were fixed at the outset: which of five scene classes (teaching, family, playing, landscape, drawing) a picture shows, if any, and which of eight object classes it contains. Three models with different architectures and training data serve as agents: an object detector, a zero-shot image--text matcher and a vision-language model. The system was built on the hypothesis that their disagreement signals doubt, and its decisions are routed under conformal risk control, which bounds the share of answers approved automatically and wrong.

The hypothesis does not hold. In a test fixed in advance, disagreement ranks the system's errors at an AUROC of $0.378$, against $0.825$ for the system's own confidence; measured within each task, disagreement reaches $0.78$ for object labels and $0.82$ for scene presence, still not above confidence ($0.84$ and $0.83$): most errors are pictures on which all models are wrong together. A detector graded against its teacher's pseudo-labels scores $0.768$ but $0.146$ against human boxes, because the teacher never labelled most classes; the thesis's own evaluator scores the same detections at $0.123$, and a better teacher raises that to $0.210$.

The thesis therefore proposes a corrected design in which each model reports what it measures and confidence decides what a person checks. For the eight object classes the models rank presence at a mean AUROC of $0.903$, and a promise on missed objects gives each picture a list of $2.8$ classes that leaves out about one object in ten ($10.8\%$ of all objects). For the five scene classes they shortlist but cannot decide: under a $5\%$ risk promise ``no scene'' is approved reliably, ``drawing'' with about one answer in six wrong, and the specific classes need a person; on random archive pictures, where playing scenes outnumber drawings, the balanced accuracy falls from $0.544$ to $0.351$. Whether a picture shows a scene at all is approved for $76.4\%$ of 1,250 labelled pictures at a $5\%$ promise, against $63.0\%$ for the system as first deployed. On 400 random archive pictures, in a test fixed in advance, that promise fails ($16.2\%$ approved and wrong), because the labels mixed two definitions of a scene and the labelled pictures are not a random sample. With one definition and calibration on random archive pictures, the risk comes to just above the promised level ($5.4$--$5.9\%$ on 294 new pictures). One vision-language model alone is about as good as the combination; for the scene classes the combination must be learned rather than fixed. The contribution is a tested account of when an automatic answer can be trusted: route by confidence, not by agreement between models; calibrate on a random sample of the collection itself; and promise what each task needs: a bound on missed objects, or a short list of scene classes.
\end{abstract}

\chapter{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
\section{The Archival Challenge in the Digital Age}
Historical illustrations are critical primary sources for historians, sociologists, 
and cultural researchers. They capture moments of social change, architectural 
evolution, and daily life that are nowhere else documented in such visual detail. 

However, the sheer volume of digitised archives makes manual cataloguing impossible.
 The Colibri Historical Image Collection contains over 53,000 images; this thesis works with 12,110 illustrations from its digitised books. Without a searchable description of what each picture shows, specific events, figures and motifs can only be found by looking through the pages.
\section{Identification of the Research Gap}
Modern object detectors perform well on benchmarks of photographs such as COCO \cite{coco2014} or OpenImages \cite{openimages2020}. Historical book illustrations differ from those photographs in kind: they are drawings and prints, scanned from aged paper. Detectors trained on photographs degrade severely on such scans (Section~\ref{sec:baseline_problem}).

A natural response is to build a restoration-and-distillation pipeline, in the standard knowledge-distillation sense: a large, slow \textit{teacher} model labels the unannotated corpus, and those \textit{pseudo-labels} (the teacher's own predictions, standing in for ground truth) are then used as training targets to \textit{distil} a small, fast \textit{student} detector (e.g.\ YOLOv11) that mimics the teacher's behaviour at a fraction of the inference cost. This pipeline was built and achieved a seemingly strong proxy mAP50 of 0.768 -- ``proxy'' here meaning the student's predictions were scored against the teacher's own pseudo-labels, not independent human ground truth. However, evaluating a student against synthetic pseudo-labels is a known methodological vulnerability; proxy metrics often mask deep epistemic failures in historical domains. Indeed, once the system was deployed across all 12,110 Colibri images, the proxy score could not answer the question that mattered for a catalogue: \textit{which} of its outputs are wrong? The working assumption at the time was that a single model's own confidence cannot be trusted to say so under a domain shift this large. This thesis tests that assumption and finds it wrong: the system's own confidence turns out to be the better warning (Section~\ref{sec:c4_preregistered}).

This observation reframed the research question. Rather than asking ``how can one model detect better?'', the question became: ``\textbf{can the disagreement between models be used as a systematic signal for identifying where AI understanding breaks down on historical images?}'' The system studied in this thesis, \textit{Dual-Core Validation and Multi-Agent Curation (DCV-MAC)}, was built on the hypothesis that it can.

The thesis tests that hypothesis and does not confirm it. On this archive, disagreement between the models ranks the system's errors no better than the system's own confidence, at the level of the picture and of the single object class (Section~\ref{sec:c4_preregistered}), and most of the corrected system's errors are pictures on which all three of its agents are wrong (Section~\ref{sec:blind_spot_features}). Agreement between models also fails in a second role, as a filter for training labels (Section~\ref{sec:label_filter}). What the thesis contributes is therefore different from what it set out to show: a measured account of what agreement between models can and cannot tell, a corrected design in which every agent reports what it measures and confidence decides what a person checks (Chapter~\ref{ch:corrected_design}), and a test of the risk guarantee on the archive itself: inside the labelled pool the corrected design approves substantially more without review than the design as first built and deployed (called the \emph{deployed design} below), but on pictures drawn at random from the archive the guarantee comes close to its promise only when the cut-off is calibrated on such pictures (Section~\ref{sec:archive_check}).

The research gap addressed in this work is twofold: (1) the lack of evaluations that grade automatically trained detectors for such archives against people rather than against the models that trained them, and (2) the absence of a principled, tested mechanism for deciding \textit{how far} an automated system may be trusted when analysing semantically ambiguous historical content.

\section{Research Objectives and Goals}
\label{sec:objectives}
The overarching goal of this research is to bridge the archival semantic gap, the distance between what a catalogue records about a picture and what the picture shows, through a reproducible \textbf{multi-agent} framework for historical indexing and validation. Specifically, this work addresses the following research questions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1 -- Uncertainty from Disagreement}: Can cross-modal agent disagreement reliably estimate epistemic uncertainty on domain-shifted visual collections?
    \item \textbf{RQ2 -- Workload Reduction}: Does the multi-agent framework reduce human curation workload without sacrificing error recall?
    \item \textbf{RQ3 -- Contradiction Detection}: Does cross-modal contradiction detection improve curation precision beyond the Tier 1 SAA validation core alone?
    \item \textbf{RQ4 -- Retrieval \& Cross-Archive Discovery}: Can the framework support archival retrieval and cross-collection discovery?
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The two tasks.} The questions are asked of two tasks whose classes were fixed at the start of this work and not changed afterwards; their written definitions, and one rule for what counts as a scene, came later (Sections~\ref{sec:scene_classes} and~\ref{sec:archive_check}). For scenes, the system decides which of five scene classes a picture shows (teaching, family, playing, landscape or drawing), or that it shows no scene. ``Drawing'' is the class for figurative content that fits none of the other four. For objects, it decides which of eight object classes a picture contains (person, child, animal, building, weapon, vehicle, tree, text), and the object finder also says where. Whether a picture shows a scene at all is the first level of the scene decision. It has the most labels, and the tests on randomly drawn archive pictures were labelled at that level.

\paragraph{What ``agent'' means here.} In the literature on multi-agent systems an agent is an autonomous component that perceives its environment and acts on it, and several agents may communicate, negotiate or coordinate \cite{wooldridge1995agents,wooldridge2009mas}. The agents of this thesis are narrower. Each is a separately trained model with its own input and its own form of output, which reports on a picture without seeing the other agents' reports; a fixed procedure, not negotiation, combines the reports, and the agents do not act on the archive. The term is kept because the system was designed and named in these terms and because each component is treated as an independent source of evidence. The research question of this thesis concerns that independence: whether reports from separate sources tell more about a picture, and about the system's own errors, than one source does.

\section{Evaluation Methodology}
The evaluation separates how well the system decides from how well it knows when it may be wrong:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Single-agent baselines}: Each agent is evaluated on its own against human labels, per object class and per scene type (Section~\ref{sec:baselines} and Chapter~\ref{ch:spatial_collapse}).
    \item \textbf{Combination against single agents}: The combined decision, for the scene classes, the object classes and scene presence, is compared with each agent alone, in ranking quality and in balanced accuracy, and fixed combination weights with learned ones (Chapter~\ref{ch:corrected_design}).
    \item \textbf{Ablation}: One agent or component is removed at a time and the system re-scored, every variant refitting its own mappings and cut-off.
    \item \textbf{Repeated book-disjoint splits}: Every headline figure of the corrected design on the labelled pool is a mean over 200 redraws of the split, with no book on two sides, so that no single split decides a result.
    \item \textbf{Errors defined by people}: Whether a decision is wrong is taken from human labels, never from the agents' own scores, wherever the design allows it.
    \item \textbf{Triage and risk control}: Disagreement-based and confidence-based triage are compared at ranking errors, and the share of pictures approved without review is measured under a stated risk level.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{How the thesis is organised.} Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} describes the architecture and the system as it was deployed. Chapter~\ref{ch:spatial_collapse} examines the single models and the pseudo-label collapse. Chapter~\ref{ch:uncertainty_calibration} analyses the disagreement signal of the deployed system, shows what it measures, and documents the correction of the evaluation labels. Chapter~\ref{ch:triage_risk_control} introduces the triage policy and risk control. Chapter~\ref{ch:corrected_design} presents the corrected design, which is the system this thesis evaluates, with its results inside the labelled pool, and Chapter~\ref{ch:corrected_tests} tests it on the archive and returns to the first research question. Chapter~\ref{ch:downstream_utility} covers the downstream agents, and Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusions} answers the research questions. Appendix~\ref{app:audit_sequence} summarises the audit of the deployed signals. Chapters~\ref{ch:methodology} to~\ref{ch:triage_risk_control} are kept short; the detailed analyses behind them are in Appendix~\ref{app:deployed_details}. Appendix~\ref{app:corrected_more} holds further analyses of the corrected design and the list of planned and added analyses; Appendices~\ref{app:example_pictures} to~\ref{app:derivations} give example pictures, two transfer pilots and the derivation of the deployed signals.

Table~\ref{tab:authoritative_cohort_taxonomy} (Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}) defines every set of pictures the thesis uses: six cohorts ($C_0$--$C_5$) for the analyses of the deployed design, and the labelled pool and random archive samples behind the corrected design and its archive test.

\section{State of the Art in Historical Image Analysis}
Recent years have seen a significant methodological shift in computational cultural heritage research. Rather than treating historical imagery as a mere variant of natural images, modern studies increasingly recognise archives as a distinct visual domain characterised by degradation, stylistic divergence, and annotation scarcity. This section situates the DCV-MAC framework within this emerging research landscape.

\subsection{Object Detection Under Historical Domain Shift}
Traditional object detectors trained on modern benchmarks such as COCO \cite{coco2014} assume high-resolution, colour-consistent imagery with stable texture statistics. Historical illustrations violate these assumptions: they are drawings and prints rather than photographs, scanned from aged paper. Consequently, detectors exhibit severe performance degradation when directly applied to archives.

Kim, Im and Mandl~\cite{kim2024historical_od} present the most directly aligned study with this thesis. They find objects in historical images without manual annotation: images from the COCO dataset are translated into the style of the historical domain with CycleGAN, and a detector is trained with items obtained by pseudo-labelling on the original and the translated COCO images. Comparing strategies for assembling the training set, their best method reaches an F1 score of $0.58$ over $15$ object types without any manual labelling.

This thesis works from the other side of the same gap. Rather than translating modern training images into the historical style, it restores the archival scans themselves (Real-ESRGAN) before they reach the models, and it adds a step their setting does not need: deciding, under a stated risk, which automatic answers a person must check. For the zero-shot scene agent, restoring the scans turns out not to matter: it ranks scenes equally well on the original scans (Section~\ref{sec:restoration_ablation}).

Two further lines of work face the same gap between photographs and depictions. Inoue et al.~\cite{inoue2018crossdomain} adapt a detector trained on photographs to clipart, watercolours and comics by the same two steps, translation into the target style and pseudo-labelling of target images, and report gains of about 5 to 20 points of mAP. Gonthier et al.~\cite{gonthier2018artworks} detect objects in paintings from image-level labels alone, including iconographic classes that cannot be learned from photographs. Both evaluate on target images annotated by people, which is the form of evaluation this thesis argues a pseudo-labelled detector needs (Chapter~\ref{ch:spatial_collapse}).

Because the work of Kim, Im and Mandl uses the two mechanisms this thesis also employs, transfer learning and pseudo-labelling, it is the closest precedent for Chapter~\ref{ch:spatial_collapse}, and this thesis's first negative result bears on it. Self-training pipelines are often validated against a held-out split of their own pseudo-labels \cite{pseudo_labeling2013,noisy_student2020}; Chapter~\ref{ch:spatial_collapse} shows that on this archive such a score measures agreement with the teacher's blind spots rather than with the historical content. This does not refute their method, which this thesis also uses, but it adds a requirement: \textbf{a pseudo-labelled detector's accuracy is only interpretable against a human reference.} Within cultural-heritage imaging more broadly, Kipke et al.~\cite{kipke2022} apply object detection to painter attribution on Greek vases, and Lengauer~\cite{lengauer2022} combines crossmodal search, visual exploration and restoration for digital pottery; both treat detection output as an entry point to curatorial work rather than as a finished answer.

\subsection{Surveyed Challenges in Visual-Art Object Detection}
\label{sec:annotation_ambiguity_note}
While historical illustrations represent one form of domain shift, visual art datasets introduce even greater depictive variability. Bengamra et al.~\cite{bengamra2024survey_visualart_od} provide a comprehensive survey of object detection methods in visual art, proposing a taxonomy organised by supervision level, detection frameworks, and representation challenges. Among the challenges they discuss, three matter here:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Depictive Domain Gap}: Differences between artistic and photographic representations.
    \item \textbf{Annotation Ambiguity}: Lack of consistent object definitions across datasets.
    \item \textbf{Metric Instability}: Difficulty evaluating detectors on stylistically diverse corpora.
\end{itemize}

Although focused primarily on paintings and artworks, these challenges map directly onto historical archives. Degradation artefacts function analogously to stylistic abstraction, distorting edge and texture features. \textit{Annotation ambiguity}, however, is not merely a cross-dataset labelling-consistency problem that a shared schema could resolve: many of the categories a historical archive needs to index (``portrait,'' ``genre scene,'' ``allegorical figure,'' ``ceremonial object'') are contested concepts within art-historical and humanities scholarship itself, with definitions that shift by period, institution, and cataloguing tradition -- the ambiguity is intrinsic to the concept, not an artefact of how any one dataset happened to label it. This is a stronger and more persistent obstacle than cross-dataset inconsistency: even a single, internally consistent archive can contain disputed category boundaries. For this thesis the consequence is that its fixed classes, set at the outset (Section~\ref{sec:objectives}), must be expected to carry disagreement between people; Section~\ref{sec:scene_agreement} measures how much, and the scene-class results are bounded by it. The same gap reaches beyond detection: pose estimators trained on photographs fail on artistic depictions \cite{schneider_vollmer_2024_popia}.

\subsection{Foundation Models and Epistemic Reframing in Digital Art History}
Recent discourse in digital humanities increasingly interrogates the epistemological implications of large-scale foundation models. Impett and Offert~\cite{impett_offert_2022_dah} argue that transformer-based vision architectures implicitly encode a cultural model of visuality derived from web-scale datasets. These models do not merely detect objects; they operationalise historically situated visual categories learned from contemporary imagery.

This perspective is critical when applying VLMs to historical archives: predictions may reflect modern semantic priors rather than historically accurate interpretations. The system therefore treats every automated description as a proposition to be checked, with a risk promise deciding which pictures a person reviews; the thesis also tests whether agreement between models can serve as that check, and finds that the system's own confidence serves better (Section~\ref{sec:c4_preregistered}).



\subsection{Multi-Agent Object Detection Paradigms}
Parallel to the rise of foundation models, recent research has explored multi-agent architectures to overcome the limitations of monolithic object detectors. Coelho et al.~\cite{coelho2023multiagent} designed a multi-agent system for multimodal machine learning object detection, highlighting the advantages of distributed, collaborative intelligence over single-model pipelines in complex environments. On this archive the thesis finds no such advantage: one vision-language model is about as good as the combination of three agents (Sections~\ref{sec:scene_classes} and~\ref{sec:objects_llava_vs_fusion}).

Outside cultural heritage, Xia et al.~\cite{xia2025dota} let collaborating agents in autonomous driving label each other's unlabelled LiDAR scans, generating and refining labels without manual annotation, a further case of one model's output serving as another's training labels, as the new teacher's boxes do in Section~\ref{sec:student_rebuild}.

\subsection{Comparison with Agent Orchestration Frameworks}
The rapid emergence of general-purpose multi-agent frameworks---such as Microsoft AutoGen \cite{autogen2024}, CrewAI \cite{crewai}, and LangGraph \cite{langgraph}---has popularised multi-agent cooperation for natural language workflows, code generation, and web navigation. Three of their properties make them a poor fit for curating a visual collection under a risk promise:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Unstructured Text Bloat}: General frameworks rely on free-form natural language exchanges between agents, whose length, cost and latency grow with every round of the conversation.
    \item \textbf{Lack of Spatial Grounding}: Text-based agent loops exchange descriptions, not boxes or masks, so they have no direct way to reconcile spatial disagreements.
    \item \textbf{Non-Deterministic Triage}: A consensus reached in conversation yields no calibrated score, so there is nothing to which a formal risk bound for human review could be attached.
\end{itemize}
The system instead uses a fixed three-tier protocol (Section~\ref{sec:tiers}): the agents exchange numbers rather than text, and a risk-controlled decision rule, not a conversation, decides which pictures a person sees; the agents that decide run offline at no API cost.

\subsection{Positioning Relative to Common GLAM Enrichment Approaches}
This section compares DCV-MAC with three enrichment approaches common in the Galleries, Libraries, Archives and Museums (GLAM) sector:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Single-model zero-shot tagging}: A common enrichment pattern across GLAM digitisation
    projects is a single zero-shot classifier or captioning model (e.g., a CLIP-family tagger) applied
    directly to scans, with no explicit uncertainty signal. This thesis does not argue against one model: a single vision-language model, calibrated and routed by its confidence, does about as well as its combination of three (Section~\ref{sec:scene_classes}). What such tagging usually lacks is a calibrated decision on which tags a person must check, and a calibration on the collection itself: a pilot on real images from the Europeana Open Culture API (10 institutions, Appendix~\ref{app:europeana}) shows the unchanged pipeline missing $65.2\%$ of confirmed scenes on another collection.
    \item \textbf{Cloud-hosted single-model APIs}: Where an enrichment pipeline depends on a cloud API for
    every image, per-call cost and data-residency constraints scale linearly with corpus size. DCV-MAC's
    Tier 1--2 validation core runs offline on a single local GPU with no per-call cost, a design choice made for this reason.
    \item \textbf{Keyword and low-level visual search}: Search built on caption keywords or global visual similarity cannot resolve queries about scene content or object co-occurrence; a 10-query pilot with the system's metadata is reported in Section~\ref{sec:retrieval_benchmark}, judged by a person on $4$ of its queries.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Epistemic Uncertainty and Domain Shift in General Computer Vision}
The challenges of model domain shift and unreliable supervision are not unique to historical collections; they are foundational open problems across applied computer vision, and two adjacent literatures are directly instructive here.

In medical image analysis, Karimi et al.~\cite{karimi2020noisy} survey the techniques and remedies available for training deep models on data whose \emph{labels} are themselves unreliable. That problem proved to be a central methodological obstacle here: a $1{,}199$-image block of the evaluation pool carried catalogue-default negative labels that had never been human-reviewed, and $57.5\%$ of a $449$-image re-audit proved wrong (Section~\ref{sec:clean_holdout}). The lesson this thesis draws, consistent with that literature, is that in a specialist domain label noise is rarely random: it follows the cataloguing process that produced it, so it is not averaged away by collecting more data and will silently invert a negative result if left unaudited. Label errors in evaluation data are common even in the standard benchmarks: Northcutt et al.~\cite{northcutt2021labelerrors} estimate an average of $3.3\%$ across the test sets of ten widely used datasets, enough to change which of two models looks better.

In remote sensing, Tuia et al.~\cite{tuia2016domain} survey domain adaptation for the classification of satellite and aerial imagery, where covariate shift arises from seasonal variation, geographic difference and sensor characteristics. The structural parallel with historical archives is close: a model trained on one acquisition regime is applied to another, and the resulting failure is typically \emph{silent} rather than announced by a low confidence score. The obvious response, measuring consensus across several models instead of trusting one model's confidence, is the hypothesis this thesis tests for computational cultural heritage; on this archive confidence turns out to be the better warning (Section~\ref{sec:c4_preregistered}).

\subsection{Zero-Shot Vision-Language Classification: Methods and Training Data}
Zero-shot classification is one of the three agents that decide, so the methods behind it matter. \textbf{CLIP} \cite{clip2021} trains an image encoder and a text encoder contrastively on 400 million web image-caption pairs, so that an image can be classified by comparing it with text embeddings of candidate class names, with no class-specific training; it reports $76.2\%$ top-1 zero-shot accuracy on ImageNet. \textbf{SigLIP} \cite{siglip2023}, the model used here (\texttt{siglip-so400m-patch14-384}), replaces CLIP's softmax over a batch with a sigmoid per image-text pair. For this thesis the important consequence is that each prompt is scored on its own, so the model can report that no prompt fits a picture, which a softmax over the prompts cannot (Section~\ref{sec:corrected_agents}). \textbf{Grounding DINO} \cite{groundingdino2023}, the new teacher of the object finder (Section~\ref{sec:student_rebuild}), extends the same open-vocabulary idea to boxes: arbitrary noun phrases are grounded to regions. Open-vocabulary models suit an archive whose categories are still contested (Section~\ref{sec:annotation_ambiguity_note}), because they can be queried with whatever vocabulary a cataloguing task needs, without retraining.

The closest recent evaluation on material of this kind is that of Piper and Jiang~\cite{piper2026illustrations}, who test five contemporary multimodal models on 1,969 children's book illustrations, over visual labelling tasks and caption generation. By their account, the strongest models perform well against human consensus annotations on concrete tasks such as illustration and setting detection, and reproduce the disagreement found between people on entities, objects and actions. This thesis meets the same limit from the other side: in the labelled pool its scene classes are recognised about as reliably as people agree on them (Section~\ref{sec:scene_classes}), and on random archive pictures less well (Section~\ref{sec:archive_check}), and the risk promise is only as trustworthy as the labels it is calibrated on.

\subsection{Synthesis: What This Thesis Takes From This Literature}
Three themes from this literature shape the design, and the thesis tests each rather than assuming it:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Teacher--student distillation for efficiency}: A large, slow model labels the archive and a small, fast detector learns from those labels, as in Kim, Im and Mandl \cite{kim2024historical_od}. The thesis keeps the approach but grades the student against people rather than against its teacher, which changes the verdict (Chapter~\ref{ch:spatial_collapse}); a better teacher then improves the student (Section~\ref{sec:student_rebuild}).
    \item \textbf{Restoration for degraded scans}: A restoration layer was meant to move archival scans closer to the images modern models were trained on; for the zero-shot scene agent it makes no measurable difference (Section~\ref{sec:restoration_ablation}). It is used for inference only, never as evidence (Section~\ref{sec:ethics}).
    \item \textbf{Structured communication instead of conversation}: The agents exchange numbers rather than text, and a risk-controlled decision rule decides what a person sees.
\end{itemize}

The literature also suggests that agreement between several models signals how far an automated description can be trusted. That is the hypothesis this thesis tests most thoroughly, and on this archive it does not hold: the system's own confidence is the better signal. What the thesis contributes is therefore a tested account of when an automated answer can be trusted, with a risk promise attached, rather than a new detector.

\section*{The Thesis at a Glance}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{The Thesis at a Glance}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The question.} When can an automatic answer about a historical illustration be trusted without a person checking it? The tasks are the five scene classes (teaching, family, playing, landscape, drawing, or no scene) and the eight object classes (person, child, animal, building, weapon, vehicle, tree, text). The system combines an object detector, a zero-shot image--text matcher and a vision-language model, and was built on the idea that their disagreement signals doubt.
    \item \textbf{Disagreement warns of mistakes no better than confidence (RQ1: no).} In a test fixed before it was run, disagreement ranks the system's errors at an AUROC of $0.378$, the system's own confidence at $0.825$. That test asked object-class disagreement about scene errors; measured within each task, disagreement reaches $0.78$ for object labels and $0.82$ for scene presence, against $0.84$ and $0.83$ for confidence. Most errors are pictures on which all agents are wrong together (Chapter~\ref{ch:uncertainty_calibration}, Section~\ref{sec:c4_preregistered} and Section~\ref{sec:blind_spot_features}).
    \item \textbf{A detector graded against its teacher looks good and is not.} A student detector scores $0.768$ against its teacher's labels and $0.146$ against human boxes, both by the detector library's own validator; the thesis's evaluator scores the same detections at $0.123$, and a better teacher raises that to $0.210$ (Chapter~\ref{ch:spatial_collapse}, Section~\ref{sec:student_rebuild}).
    \item \textbf{Object classes: ranked well, promised as a short list.} The models rank pictures containing each of the eight classes above those without it at a mean AUROC of $0.903$. A promise on missed objects gives each picture a list of $2.8$ classes that leaves out $9.2\%$ of a picture's objects on average, as promised, and $10.8\%$ of all objects, because pictures without objects count as nothing missed; single objects cannot be confirmed automatically, and boxes for the rare classes need a person (Section~\ref{sec:objects_llava_vs_fusion}).
    \item \textbf{Scene classes: shortlisted, not decided.} The agents' combination has to be learned: with the inherited fixed weights the balanced accuracy is $0.401$, learned it is $0.544$. Under a $5\%$ risk promise ``no scene'' is approved reliably and ``drawing'' with about one answer in six wrong; the few family or landscape answers approved are often wrong, so the specific classes go to a person. With the classes weighted equally (an analysis added after the plan), a short list contains the right class for $80$--$94\%$ of the family, playing and landscape pictures. People themselves agree on these classes only moderately ($\kappa = 0.337$; Section~\ref{sec:scene_classes}). On random archive pictures the scenes are mostly playing, not drawing, and the mapping learned on the pool reaches a balanced accuracy of only $0.351$ (Section~\ref{sec:archive_check}).
    \item \textbf{Scene presence, the first level (RQ2: yes, with a condition).} Inside the 1,250 labelled pictures the corrected design approves $76.4\%$ without review at a promised risk of $5\%$, against $63.0\%$ for the deployed design's triage score and $61.1\%$ for routing by its disagreement alone. On randomly drawn archive pictures a cut-off calibrated on the labelled pictures lets $16.2\%$ through approved and wrong; with one definition of a scene and calibration on random archive pictures, the risk comes to just above the promised level ($5.4$--$5.9\%$ at about $80\%$ approved; Section~\ref{sec:archive_check}).
    \item \textbf{One model against several, and against the baselines.} The learned combination is slightly ahead of the vision-language model alone on the scene classes (balanced accuracy $0.544$ against $0.507$, not established) and level with it on objects. Against the old system, the corrected design ranks the eight object classes far better (mean AUROC $0.903$ against $0.624$) and approves $13.5$ points more scene-presence calls at a $5\%$ promise, both established; it is not shown to name the scene class better ($82.1\%$ against $77.4\%$ correct, not established). Against a linear probe on one model's image embedding, the standard single-model baseline, it is clearly better on objects ($0.903$ against $0.838$) and level on both scene tasks (Section~\ref{sec:against_baselines}, Figure~\ref{fig:summary_baselines}).
    \item \textbf{For an archive adopting this}, the practical rule is: route by confidence, calibrate on a random sample of the archive's own pictures labelled under the definition it means to apply (reweighting unlabelled pictures does not replace this), and promise what the task needs, a bound on missed objects for objects and a short list for scene classes.
\end{itemize}

\chapter{Methodology and System Architecture}
\label{ch:methodology}

This chapter defines the Dual-Core Validation and Multi-Agent Curation (DCV-MAC) framework, the system as it was deployed.

\paragraph{Which design this chapter describes.} This chapter and Chapters~\ref{ch:spatial_collapse} to~\ref{ch:triage_risk_control} describe and analyse the system \emph{as it was deployed}: four signals, among them a derived Agreement score, combined into the disagreement proxy $U_{\text{SAA}}$. That analysis is the evidence for the thesis's negative finding and is kept in full. It is not the system this thesis proposes. The corrected design, in which the Agreement score is removed, the text reader takes no part in the decision, and confidence rather than disagreement decides what a person checks, is presented in Chapter~\ref{ch:corrected_design}; Table~\ref{tab:what_changed} lists every difference. A reader who wants the proposed system first can read Sections~\ref{sec:tiers} and~\ref{sec:cohort_taxonomy} and go on to Chapter~\ref{ch:corrected_design}; Chapters~\ref{ch:spatial_collapse} to~\ref{ch:triage_risk_control} give the evidence for why it was replaced.

\paragraph{Why not a simpler approach first.} Two simpler alternatives were tried first. A single COCO-pretrained detector applied as-is separates pictures containing a person, an animal or a vehicle at only $\text{ROC-AUC}=0.79$, $0.74$ and $0.75$ (Section~\ref{sec:baseline_problem}). A restoration-and-distillation pipeline (teacher pseudo-labels $\to$ student detector) looks successful by its own validation metric ($\text{mAP}_{50}=0.768$) but collapses against human ground truth ($0.146$, Chapter~\ref{ch:spatial_collapse}), and nothing inside the pipeline flagged the failure. Since a single model has no independent signal to check itself against, the deployed design replaced it with \emph{several} independent models whose disagreement was to stand in for that missing signal. That is the hypothesis the design was built on; Chapter~\ref{ch:uncertainty_calibration} and Section~\ref{sec:c4_preregistered} test it and do not confirm it.

\section{3-Tier Decoupled Architecture}
\label{sec:tiers}
Rather than treating all model components as voting members in an unconstrained chat loop, DCV-MAC establishes a strict \textbf{3-Tier Decoupled Protocol}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Tier 1 (Validation Core / Multi-Model Ensemble)}: Comprises the primary vision and vision-language models whose cross-modal consensus directly calculates the Scene-Aware Agreement ($SAA_i$) metric and operational triage score ($U_{\text{triage}, i}$):
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Object detector}, the Spatial Grounding Agent (YOLOv11-seg + SAM): bounding boxes and instance masks from YOLOv11 \cite{yolov11_2024} and SAM \cite{sam2023}, for the spatial analyses of Chapter~\ref{ch:spatial_collapse}. Chosen because it is small, self-hostable and fast ($0.06$\,s per image with loading and post-processing), which makes corpus-scale inference and distillation tractable. In the deployed run the scalar $A_i^{\text{object}}$ did not come from this agent (Section~\ref{sec:canonical_saa}).
        \item \textit{Scene matcher}, a zero-shot scene classifier (SigLIP): scene probabilities against natural-language prompts \cite{siglip2023}. Chosen because it matches images to arbitrary text rather than a fixed label set, so the archive's historically contingent categories (Section~\ref{sec:annotation_ambiguity_note}) need no retraining. In the deployed run $A_i^{\text{scene}}$ was read from the vision-language model instead (Section~\ref{sec:canonical_saa}).
        \item \textit{Describer}, a visual reasoning agent (LLaVA-OneVision 7B): visual question answering, scene descriptions and object lists \cite{llava_onevision2024} ($A_i^{\text{vlm}}$). Chosen because an open-weight model can be queried on all 12,110 pictures at no marginal cost.
        \item \textit{Text reader}, a grounded inscription engine (Kosmos-2.5): OCR that ties each string to a bounding box \cite{kosmos2_5_2024}, which the inscription reader (Section~\ref{sec:agent5_ocr}) needs to check \emph{where} an inscription appears.
    \end{itemize}
    A fifth, \emph{derived} signal, \textit{Agreement} between the spatial and visual-reasoning agents, is the fourth term (with Object, Scene and VLM) of the disagreement proxy $U_{\text{SAA}}$ (Section~\ref{sec:canonical_saa}).

    \paragraph{Which object and scene classes are covered.} The detector was trained on ten object classes. Every evaluation in this thesis reports the eight that the human boxes and the detector share: \textit{person, child, animal, building, weapon, vehicle, tree, text} (horses are merged into \textit{animal}; the rater's \textit{furniture} and \textit{hat} boxes never reached the label files). The scene classifier is zero-shot, but it is evaluated against five scene types: \textit{drawing, landscape, family, playing, teaching}.

    \item \textbf{Tier 2 (Downstream Historiographic Curation Tier)}: Specialised downstream modules that read the stored outputs of the Tier 1 agents for every picture, whatever its route, to extract historiographic insights without touching the Tier 1 decision. They are named by what they do (in the system's code and result files they are agents 2, 4 and 5):
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Contradiction checker}: flags objects the vision-language model names that the detector does not find (Section~\ref{sec:agent2_contradiction}).
        \item \textit{Cross-archive linker}: finds pictures in other books whose descriptions or embeddings are close, to surface reprinted or recurring motifs (Section~\ref{sec:agent4_linking}).
        \item \textit{Inscription reader}: reads inscriptions with Kosmos-2.5 and ties each string to a region of the picture (Section~\ref{sec:agent5_ocr}).
    \end{itemize}
    \begin{flushleft}
    \textbf{Architectural Decoupling Rule}: Tier 2 agents read only what Tier 1 has stored and write only flags and links for curation. They do not participate in the core $SAA$ calculation or the routing, preventing feedback loops and mathematical circularity.
    \end{flushleft}
    
    \item \textbf{Tier 3 (designed, not run)}: An optional escalation of the pictures Tiers 1--2 cannot resolve to two independent commercial models, one proposing and one checking. It was not run in this thesis, because evaluating it was outside the project's budget; those pictures go to a person instead.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Design Rationale: An Escalation Ladder, Not a Single Model.} The decision climbs a ladder ordered by cost: Tier~1 is free and deterministic, Tier~3 incurs commercial API cost, and human review is the most expensive step. Each step receives only the pictures the previous one could not resolve. Tier~2 is not a step on the ladder: it runs on every picture beside the decision, and keeping it out of the $SAA$ calculation prevents an agent's own output from influencing the score that decides whether that output is trusted. The deployed design used $U_{\text{SAA}}$ to decide how far up the ladder a picture travels; Chapter~\ref{ch:uncertainty_calibration} shows that it does not rank errors better than confidence, and the corrected design of Chapter~\ref{ch:corrected_design} routes by confidence on the same ladder.

The complete system topology and operational dataflow of the DCV-MAC framework are illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:full_pipeline_flow}.

\paragraph{Annotation methodology.} $C_1$ and $C_2$ carry human ground truth from different protocols. $C_1$ ($n=801$) was labelled in CVAT by a domain expert who reviewed and corrected an automated scene-presence pass, giving each picture one of the five scene classes or none; the scene-class task of Chapter~\ref{ch:corrected_design} and its first-level \textit{has\_scene} label come from it. $C_2$ ($n=300$) holds bounding boxes and one scene class per picture, given by the author in a separate task that showed only the raw image, independently of the $C_1$ labels.

\section{Generative Restoration Layer and Methodological Discipline}
Archival scans show paper ageing, fading and printing artefacts. Before feature extraction, every one of the $12{,}110$ corpus scans ($\mathbf{I}_{\text{raw}}$) is restored once by Real-ESRGAN \cite{realesrgan2021} (its $4\times$ model, written out at twice the original size); all agents in this thesis see that restored image ($\mathbf{I}_{\text{refined}}$). A fuller generative chain was also built -- CycleGAN domain translation \cite{cyclegan2017} $\rightarrow$ Real-ESRGAN $\rightarrow$ Stable Diffusion Img2Img refinement \cite{stable_diffusion2022} ($\text{strength}=0.35$) -- and applied to a subset of $231$ scans; it was not run over the corpus.

\begin{flushleft}
\textbf{Archival integrity}: The restored image ($\mathbf{I}_{\text{refined}}$) is an input for the models only. It is never stored or presented as historical evidence, and all catalogue metadata refers to the original scans ($\mathbf{I}_{\text{raw}}$).
\end{flushleft}

Keeping restoration at the input layer protects primary catalogue records from anything the restoration invents. A per-image Laplacian-variance sharpness measurement over all $12{,}110$ scans (raw scan against its Real-ESRGAN output) shows that restoration increases structural edge sharpness in $83.8\%$ of them. Sharper edges do not mean better answers: for the zero-shot scene agent, the original scans give the same results as the restored ones (Section~\ref{sec:restoration_ablation}), so no gain in detection or classification is claimed for restoration. An example of the restoration chain is shown in Section~\ref{sec:moved_restoration}.


\section{Canonical Formulation of Scene-Aware Agreement (SAA)}
\label{sec:canonical_saa}
The four levels below are \emph{not} stages of one computation: Levels 2, 3 and 4 are three scalars built from overlapping subsets of the agent outputs, for classification, uncertainty and routing. Several other quantities in this thesis draw on different subsets again; Table~\ref{tab:signal_sets} in Appendix~\ref{app:derivations} states every signal set in one place.


\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Level 1 (Agent Output Vector)}: For an image $i$, the Tier 1 agents produce four signals in $[0,1]$ -- spatial object grounding $A_i^{\text{object}}$, zero-shot scene $A_i^{\text{scene}}$, VLM description $A_i^{\text{vlm}}$, and grounded inscription $A_i^{\text{ocr}}$. Restoration quality, a further measured signal, enters only AWLF and the deep-ensemble baseline; with it and the derived Agreement term below, AWLF reads six agent signals. Two of the four are \emph{saturating counts} rather than model confidences, and stating the actual constructions matters for how the spread over them should be read:
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
A_i^{\text{object}} &= \min\!\left(\tfrac{\text{VQA object count}}{3},\, 1\right) && \in \{0,\tfrac13,\tfrac23,1\} \\
A_i^{\text{scene}}  &= \text{VQA scene confidence, clipped to } [0,1] && \in \{0.5,\,1\} \text{ in practice} \\
A_i^{\text{vlm}}    &= \text{VQA object-presence score} && \in \{0,1\} \\
A_i^{\text{ocr}}    &= \min\!\left(\tfrac{\text{characters of Kosmos-2.5 output}}{80},\, 1\right) &&
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
$A_i^{\text{ocr}}$ is therefore a measure of \emph{how much} text the OCR model emitted, not of how confident or correct that text is -- a distinction that matters given Section~\ref{sec:agent5_ocr}'s finding that $47.5\%$ of the ``candidate real OCR'' bucket contains no legible text at all, the model having produced fluent but fabricated markdown. A length-based signal scores such output high. $A_i^{\text{ocr}}$ does not enter $SAA$, $U_{\text{SAA}}$ or the triage score; it participates only in the AWLF feature vector (Section~\ref{sec:awlf}), the six-agent deep-ensemble baseline, and the pairwise correlation matrix. 

A fifth signal, \textit{Agreement}, is \emph{derived} rather than measured:
    \begin{equation}
        A_i^{\text{agree}} = 1 - \left|A_i^{\text{object}} - A_i^{\text{vlm}}\right|
        \label{eq:agree}
    \end{equation}
    It quantifies concordance between the spatial and VQA agents specifically. It is not a fifth model, and Caution 1 (Section~\ref{app:deployed_cautions}) explains why its derived status matters.

    \item \textbf{Level 2 (Scene-Aware Agreement Score)}: The classification fusion score $SAA_i$ predicts scene presence, and thresholding it at $0.5$ against the human label defines the ``error'' target for the uncertainty evaluations of the deployed design (with the exception in Caution~3). It is a weighted linear combination over three agents:
    \begin{equation}
        SAA_i = w_o A_i^{\text{object}} + w_s A_i^{\text{scene}} + w_v A_i^{\text{vlm}},
        \qquad w_o = 0.6,\; w_s = 0.2,\; w_v = 0.2
    \end{equation}
    Caution 3 (Section~\ref{app:deployed_cautions}) records an important qualification: a second fusion column exists in the pipeline, and it -- not this one -- defines the error label behind two of the thesis's headline numbers.

    \item \textbf{Level 3 (Disagreement Uncertainty Proxy)}: Level 2 answers \emph{what} is in the image; Level 3 answers \emph{how much the agents disagree about it}, and is the quantity every error-detection figure for the deployed design is computed from. The two are separate by construction -- a weighted mean over three signals versus a spread over four -- so $U_{\text{SAA},i}$ is not a rescaling of $SAA_i$ and in particular $U_{\text{SAA},i} \ne 1 - SAA_i$:
    \begin{equation}
        U_{\text{SAA}, i} = \operatorname{std}\left(A_i^{\text{object}},\ A_i^{\text{agree}},\ A_i^{\text{scene}},\ A_i^{\text{vlm}}\right)
    \end{equation}
    Cautions 1, 2 and 4 (Section~\ref{app:deployed_cautions}) apply to this definition.

    \item \textbf{Level 4 (Operational Triage Score)}: The score that routes a picture: it is approved automatically when the score is below the conformal cut-off $\hat\lambda$, and otherwise goes to a person. The $0.6/0.4$ split was set by hand; on the labelled pool every weight from $0$ to $0.6$ on the spread gives the same decisions, because the deployed signals take only eight states (Section~\ref{sec:corrected_results}). This thesis defines two triage scores sharing the same $0.6/0.4$ structural form but built from different second terms. Only one is the quantity the deployed routing and its conformal threshold $\hat\lambda$ actually use.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \emph{SCI-based} (Section~\ref{sec:complexity_triage}), with the Spatial Complexity Index $\text{SCI}_i$ (object density, independent of agent confidence), a negative result that performs close to chance and is not deployed:
        \begin{equation}
            U_{\text{triage}, i}^{\text{SCI}} = 0.6 \cdot U_{\text{SAA}, i} + 0.4 \cdot (1 - \text{SCI}_i)
        \end{equation}
        
        \item \emph{Mean-confidence-based}, with $\bar{c}_i$, the mean of the four agent signals of $U_{\text{SAA}, i}$, in place of $\text{SCI}_i$:
        \begin{equation}
            U_{\text{triage}, i}^{\text{CRC}} = 0.6 \cdot \sigma_{\text{agents}, i} + 0.4 \cdot (1 - \bar{c}_i), \quad \sigma_{\text{agents}, i} = U_{\text{SAA}, i}
        \end{equation}
        This is the score conformal risk control thresholds ($\hat\lambda=0.5370$ in-sample on $C_1$; $0.3720$ on the corrected group-level calibration at $\alpha=0.05$; Algorithm~\ref{alg:conformal_risk_control}).
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\section{An Error Meta-Classifier}
The deployed design also included a supervised meta-classifier, the Agreement-Weighted Label Filter (AWLF), which predicts the pipeline's errors from six agent signals; because Agreement is derived from Object and VLM, which share one source, the six carry about four separate measurements. Despite its name it does not filter training labels; whether agreement can do that is tested in Section~\ref{sec:label_filter}. On the corrected held-out partition ($n=249$) it reaches $\text{ROC-AUC} = 0.8702$, with the caveat that its error label is a threshold on a fusion of the same scores it reads. Its definition, the class-imbalance pathology of its first version, and its evaluation are in Section~\ref{sec:awlf}.

\section{The Pseudo-Label Collapse Paradox: \texorpdfstring{$0.768 \rightarrow 0.146$}{0.768 to 0.146}}
\label{sec:collapse_paradox}
To train deployable student detectors without manual annotation, common self-training pipelines \cite{pseudo_labeling2013,noisy_student2020} leverage vision-language teacher ensembles to generate pseudo-labels. When evaluated against the automated teacher pseudo-labels on a held-out validation split, the distilled student model achieves seemingly strong performance:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pseudo-Label Self-Consistency mAP50}: \textbf{0.768}
    \item \textbf{Pseudo-Label Self-Consistency mAP50-95}: \textbf{0.723}
\end{itemize}

\begin{flushleft}
\textbf{The Spatial Grounding Collapse}: When the exact same distilled student detector is audited against human expert annotations on the Spatial Audit Cohort $C_2$ ($n=300$), true human mAP50 collapses to \textbf{0.146} (representative detector output in Figure~\ref{fig:yolo_detection_examples})---an $81.0\%$ relative performance drop. Both figures belong to the one deployed detector: its pseudo-label score is the best validation epoch of its own training run, and its human score is measured on the $300$ hand-annotated pictures. Both come from the same evaluator, the validator of the detector library (Ultralytics), and the human score covers the eight classes that have human boxes (person $0.390$, animal $0.398$, child $0.152$, tree $0.107$, building $0.082$, weapon $0.041$, vehicle and text $0$). Section~\ref{sec:student_rebuild} compares detectors with the thesis's own evaluator, which matches boxes in order of confidence and averages precision over every recall level; on the same detections and the same eight classes it gives $0.123$, and $0.106$ on the $164$ pictures that were never in the detector's training set, the cleaner baseline because the other $136$ were. The two figures differ by evaluator, not by data, and each comparison in the thesis stays within one evaluator.
\end{flushleft}

This discrepancy exposes the \textbf{Pseudo-Label Collapse Paradox}: high pseudo-label validation accuracy measures agreement with the teacher, not accuracy on the historical content. The mechanism is omission rather than invention: of $128$ hand-drawn objects the student misses on pictures the teacher labelled, $126$ were never in the teacher's labels at all, and the teacher labelled no child, building, weapon, vehicle or text anywhere, with $88.4\%$ of its boxes persons. A student graded against such a teacher is rewarded for missing what the teacher missed. Section~\ref{sec:student_rebuild} replaces the teacher and shows the student improve.


\section{The Bounding-Box Error Paradox and a Meta-Learner Test}
\label{sec:box_error_paradox}
Evaluating spatial uncertainty reveals a secondary pathology: \textbf{the Bounding-Box Error Paradox}. Traditional heuristics assume that bounding box spatial IoU or detector confidence scores predict labelling accuracy. On the Spatial Audit Cohort $C_2$, the deployed triage score ranks the pictures the rater boxed but marked as uncertain ($32$ of $300$) at $\text{ROC-AUC} = 0.3965$---worse than random guessing.


A meta-classifier trained to find these pictures does only modestly better, and partly re-reads its own target (Section~\ref{sec:moved_meta}).

\section{Throughput and Transfer Pilots}
\label{sec:throughput_transfer}
\paragraph{Throughput.} Measured while running every model over all $12{,}110$ images on an NVIDIA RTX A6000, a picture takes $0.06$\,s in the distilled detector, $1.56$\,s in the vision-language model and $3.23$\,s in grounded OCR. Inference is not the constraint on scaling; labels are.

\paragraph{Transfer.} Two pilots look beyond the Colibri archive (Appendices~\ref{app:peopleart} and~\ref{app:europeana}). Across $43$ artistic styles in PeopleArt ($n=4{,}778$), detection degrades gracefully ($F_1$ from $0.878$ on photographic styles to $0.673$ on cartoons). On $23$ labelled images from ten other institutions via Europeana, the unchanged pipeline misses $65.2\%$ of confirmed scenes, and the ensemble there carries only $1.37$ independent signals, too few for disagreement to be measured. Neither pilot shows transfer of error detection.

\section{A Precondition for Disagreement-Based Uncertainty}
\label{sec:heterogeneity_precondition}
The Europeana result above is not only a negative transfer finding; it identifies a checkable precondition that any disagreement-based uncertainty method must satisfy on a new domain, and which can be tested \emph{before} any ground truth is collected.

Simpler diagnostics, such as how many values an agent takes or how strongly the fusion follows one agent, do not separate the two regimes, and the measure below does not depend on the derived Agreement term (Section~\ref{sec:moved_precondition}).

\paragraph{What does separate the two regimes.} The distinguishing quantity is not how many values an agent takes but how much \emph{variance} it contributes, and how many independent directions the agents jointly span. Let $\Sigma$ be the covariance matrix of the four Level-1 agent signals (Object, Agreement, Scene, VLM) over the target cohort, with eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \dots \lambda_4$ and $p_i = \lambda_i / \sum_j \lambda_j$. The ensemble is summarised by the \textbf{effective rank}~\cite{roy2007effective}, the perplexity of that spectrum:
\begin{equation}
    \text{eff-rank}(\Sigma) = \exp\left(-\sum_{i} p_i \log p_i\right)
    \label{eq:effective_rank}
\end{equation}
Equation~\ref{eq:effective_rank} equals $4$ for four uncorrelated agents of equal variance and approaches $1$ when they collapse onto a single varying signal. A near-constant agent contributes a vanishing eigenvalue and correctly lowers the score.

\paragraph{Measured values.} On the Colibri deployment corpus ($C_0$, $n=12{,}110$), where every headline result in this thesis was obtained, $\text{eff-rank}=2.50$ ($95\%$ CI $2.48$--$2.51$). On the Europeana pilot ($C_5$, $n=24$), where the error-detection question proved unanswerable, $\text{eff-rank}=1.37$ ($95\%$ CI $1.06$--$1.61$). The intervals are far apart, and the mechanism is visible in a single agent: the VLM contributes a standard deviation of $0.051$ on Europeana against $0.320$ on Colibri. Below roughly two independent directions, any disagreement statistic computed over the ensemble is a re-expression of one number. The threshold of two rests on two cohorts, one of them $24$ pictures, and on signals on different scales; it is a provisional heuristic, not an established cut-off, and would need testing on further archives.

\paragraph{A qualification: Colibri passes only on the deployed signals.} The values above are computed on the deployed Level-1 signals, which look heterogeneous partly because they sit on very different scales (a count divided by three, a binary flag, a stored $0.5$ or $1$). When each agent's own output is mapped onto a common scale, the chance of a scene, the corrected signals carry an effective rank of $1.37$ on all $12{,}110$ Colibri pictures (by coincidence the same value as on Europeana), or $1.67$ with the absence score added; the object and VLM signals alone correlate at $r=0.92$. Both values lie below the $2.0$ threshold. Colibri therefore does not pass the precondition once its signals are measured on one scale, and the precondition should be read as a necessary check on scaled signals, not as evidence that the deployed ensemble was multi-modal.

\paragraph{How to use it.} The check needs agent scores only, no labels, and returns pass or fail against a threshold of $2.0$, between the two measured regimes. If a new archive fails it, the agents should be recalibrated or replaced before annotations are collected for an error-detection figure that would be an artefact. Passing is necessary, not sufficient: the deployed Colibri signals pass, and disagreement still does not rank errors better than confidence there (Chapter~\ref{ch:uncertainty_calibration}).

\chapter{Multi-Agent Uncertainty Core and Probabilistic Calibration}
\label{ch:uncertainty_calibration}

This chapter analyses the uncertainty signal of the deployed design: its formulation, its calibration and how well it predicts errors. The signal reaches a high headline figure, but the chapter shows that the figure is not due to disagreement between the agents. It is carried by a term derived from two of the other signals, a plain confidence score matches it, and on confirmed negatives the signal falls below chance, for a largely arithmetic reason (Section~\ref{sec:clean_holdout}).

\section{Agent Disagreement as an Uncertainty Proxy}
Single-model vision architectures output overconfident probability scores under domain shift. DCV-MAC treats disagreement between spatial grounding (YOLOv11-seg), zero-shot scene classification (SigLIP) and visual-language reasoning (LLaVA-OneVision) as a proxy for \emph{epistemic} uncertainty (as designed; in the deployed run the object and scene signals were read from the vision-language model, Section~\ref{sec:canonical_saa}), which additional evidence could reduce, as distinct from irreducible \emph{aleatoric} noise \cite{kendall2017uncertainties}: a scan the models were not trained to interpret is what a human reviewer can resolve.

\paragraph{Relation to committee- and ensemble-based uncertainty.} Disagreement as an uncertainty signal goes back to query-by-committee \cite{seung1992qbc}; \textit{deep ensembles} \cite{deepensembles2017} are its standard modern form and a strong baseline against MC~Dropout \cite{gal2016dropout}. A deep ensemble varies initialisation and data order within one architecture, corpus and objective, so its members share every inductive bias. The Tier~1 ensemble instead varies architecture, modality, pre-training corpus and objective at once (a supervised detector, a contrastive image--text encoder and an autoregressive vision-language model), although in the deployed run two of the signals came from one model (Section~\ref{sec:canonical_saa}). The hypothesis tested is that such heterogeneity decorrelates errors more than within-architecture ensembling.

That hypothesis appears to hold on scene-positive content, where the deployed signal reaches a high ROC-AUC, but Section~\ref{sec:signal_decomposition} shows that this figure is not produced by disagreement, and on confirmed true negatives the signal falls below chance for a largely arithmetic reason (Section~\ref{sec:clean_holdout}). The corrected design, with three separately measured agents, shows the underlying problem directly: on $149$ of its $199$ errors all three agents are wrong as well (Section~\ref{sec:blind_spot_features}). Architectural heterogeneity is therefore not sufficient for error decorrelation: where a bias comes from the source material rather than from a shared architecture, a cross-modal ensemble agrees just as confidently as a homogeneous one. Section~\ref{sec:heterogeneity_precondition} states the corresponding precondition.

Related work on uncertainty under dataset shift and on disagreement as an estimate of error \cite{ovadia2019trust,jiang2022disagreement,baek2022agreement} is discussed in Section~\ref{sec:moved_shift}; it estimates how many pictures a population of models gets wrong, whereas the question here is which picture is wrong.

Evaluating on the $C_1$ Expert Reference Cohort ($n=801$ human-annotated images), the raw Scene-Aware Agreement uncertainty proxy $U_{\text{SAA},i}$, the spread of the four agent signals (Section~\ref{sec:canonical_saa}), achieves an error-detection performance of:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{SAA disagreement proxy}: \textbf{ROC-AUC = 0.9791}
    \item Single-Model Temperature-Scaled Entropy Baseline: $\text{ROC-AUC} = 0.6679$
\end{itemize}
This is a $46.6\%$ relative improvement over that baseline. Section~\ref{sec:signal_decomposition} examines what is actually driving it, and finds that the improvement is not attributable to cross-modal disagreement in the way this framing suggests.

\section{What the Disagreement Signal Actually Measures}
\label{sec:signal_decomposition}
The result above invites a specific reading: that \emph{disagreement between heterogeneous models} is what detects errors. This section tests that reading directly and reports that the evidence does not support it.

\paragraph{A dependency inside the estimator.} One of the four terms of $U_{\text{SAA}}$ is $A^{\text{agree}} = 1 - |A^{\text{object}} - A^{\text{vlm}}|$, an identity that holds exactly on all $12{,}110$ images, so the estimator has four terms but only three free inputs, and on this corpus only two ($A^{\text{object}}$, which also fixes $A^{\text{vlm}}$, and $A^{\text{scene}}$; Section~\ref{sec:canonical_saa}), and an asymmetry. When Object and VLM agree that nothing is present the derived term goes to $1$ and becomes a maximal outlier: the unanimous-absent states yield $U_{\text{SAA}}=0.479$ and $0.577$, the two highest values the estimator attains, while the unanimous-present states yield $0.250$ or $0$, and the $1{,}403$ images on which both report nothing carry a mean $U_{\text{SAA}}$ of $0.492$ against a corpus mean of $0.201$. \textbf{The estimator reports near-maximal uncertainty precisely where the ensemble is unanimous, provided the unanimous verdict is negative.}

\paragraph{The derived term carries the result.} Removing it and computing the standard deviation over the three measured agents drops in-sample error-detection ROC-AUC from $\mathbf{0.9791}$ to $\mathbf{0.7466}$ ($95\%$ CI $[0.711, 0.782]$). Cross-agent disagreement, measured over signals that are actually independent measurements, is therefore a substantially weaker error detector than the headline figure indicates.

\paragraph{A confidence baseline reproduces the result.} Because the derived term is large exactly when the agents' scores are low, $U_{\text{SAA}}$ correlates at $r=0.91$ with $1-\bar{A}$, a plain confidence score carrying no disagreement information whatsoever. Table~\ref{tab:signal_decomposition} compares four uncertainty signals on both cohorts.

The thesis already reported that $U_{\text{SAA}}$ ties the strongest single-model baseline on discrimination ($0.9791$ vs.\ $0.9767$). What Table~\ref{tab:signal_decomposition} adds is that a baseline with \emph{no multi-agent component at all} ties it as well.

\paragraph{A circularity in the error label.} The error label is $\mathbb{I}[S_{\text{fusion}} \ge 0.5] \ne y$, where $S_{\text{fusion}}$ is a weighted mean of the same agent scores every uncertainty signal above is computed from; the fusion column behind the in-sample label even contains the derived Agreement term (weight $0.267$). Within the scene-positive subset ``error'' is then identical to ``$S_{\text{fusion}} < 0.5$'', so any monotone proxy for low agent scores recovers the label almost by construction, and pure confidence attains ROC-AUC $=1.000$ there. A high error-detection AUROC on this task is therefore weaker evidence than it appears, for \emph{every} signal compared here.

\paragraph{What this does and does not overturn.} It does not affect the conformal risk guarantee, which holds for every score on pictures like the calibration pictures (Section~\ref{sec:crc_distribution_free}; Section~\ref{sec:archive_check} shows what happens on pictures that are not), nor the pseudo-label collapse, the label-integrity correction, the Tier~2 benchmarks or the PeopleArt evaluation. It does mean that the claim ``cross-modal disagreement is what detects the errors'' is \textbf{not established by the evidence assembled here}, and this thesis does not make it (Section~\ref{sec:not_claim}).

Four further attempts to overturn this conclusion, among them giving the ensemble back two unused signals and removing agents one at a time, leave it standing (Section~\ref{sec:moved_four}).

\subsection{Weights and Formulations}
Three further analyses, set out in Appendix~\ref{app:deployed_details}, close the obvious ways around this result. No weighting of the fusion helps: the deployed weights' apparent advantage is one cell of $276$ images whose stored $A^{\text{object}} = 0.333333$ makes it evaluate to $0.4999998$, and weights fitted on the training partition do no better on test, $0.7720$ against $0.7802$ in balanced accuracy (Section~\ref{sec:weight_sensitivity}). Nine formulations of the uncertainty score, varying the signals, the weighting and the operator, all rank errors below plain confidence against a non-circular error label (Section~\ref{sec:formulation_search}). Against the deployed error label, because the deployed signals take only eight joint values, no score over them can exceed $\text{ROC-AUC} = 0.8871$ on the held-out partition, and fused confidence ($1 - SAA$) reaches $0.8828$. Correcting the formulation step by step lowers its ROC-AUC at every step, from $0.860$ to $0.608$ (Section~\ref{sec:formulation_repair}). For the deployed pipeline the score to threshold is therefore plain confidence; doing better requires agents that fail on different pictures, not a different formula over the agents present.

\paragraph{Rebuilt signals.} Re-deriving four continuous, separately sourced signals, and holding one model out so that the error is not defined by the agents themselves, does not change the conclusion: disagreement does not outperform confidence in any informative round (Section~\ref{sec:signal_rebuild}).


\section{Held-Out Validation and a Data-Integrity Correction}
\label{sec:clean_holdout}
The $\text{ROC-AUC}=0.9791$ result above is computed on the $C_1$ cohort ($n=801$), which is $99.4\%$ scene-positive by construction (only $5$ negative-control images) and was also used across weight selection, calibration, and AWLF development. To obtain a methodologically clean estimate, the author constructed a publication-level (PPN) held-out split over a wider $2{,}000$-image labelling pool: the $801$-image $C_1$ cohort plus $1{,}199$ further catalogue records nominally labelled ``no scene present.''

\paragraph{A data-integrity discovery.} The $1{,}199$-image segment had zero label variation: every row carried no scene and no box, with nothing to document how it was produced, which is consistent with catalogue records defaulted to ``no scene'' rather than with human review. A random sample of $144$ of them, reviewed by the author, showed \textbf{$96/144$ ($66.7\%$, $95\%$ CI $59$--$74\%$) to be false negatives}: pictures that plainly show a scene. Only pure text, sheet music and non-representational ornament were reliably true negatives.

\paragraph{Rebuilding the evaluation pool.} The never-reviewed images were discarded (their labels are unknown, not negative) and as many as feasible were re-verified instead, by the author in two passes of $144$ and $305$ images with no overlap. Of the $449$, $258$ ($57.5\%$) were false negatives. All of these labels come from one person, so individual borderline pictures may be labelled differently by another rater. The corrected pool is the $801$ CVAT-reviewed $C_1$ images plus these $449$: $1{,}250$ images, $84.3\%$ positive, $196$ confirmed negatives (a third pass over $285$ further pictures, outside the pool, added $123$ more, $319$ in all; Section~\ref{sec:moved_negctrl}). It was split by publication into train ($60\%$, $n=749$), calibration ($20\%$, $n=252$) and test ($20\%$, $n=249$, of which $38$ are confirmed negatives), with no book or image on two sides. All numbers below are test-only.

\paragraph{The corrected result reverses the original finding, and the overall estimate rests on all $249$ verified test images rather than on the $38$-image negative-control subgroup alone.} On the original, contaminated $2{,}000$-image split, clean-test SAA disagreement AUROC was $0.393$ overall -- apparently below chance. On the corrected $1{,}250$-image pool, the identical computation gives $\text{ROC-AUC}=0.860$ -- below the in-sample $0.9791$ figure, but well above chance and consistent with out-of-sample generalisation, if imperfect. Table~\ref{tab:negative_control_gap} decomposes both by true class.

\paragraph{The same partition, against the two standard uncertainty baselines.} Two baselines that the literature would reach for first were run on the corrected partition alongside the deployed proxy. Temperature-scaled single-model entropy reaches $\text{ROC-AUC}=0.7008$ ($\text{ECE}=0.0442$) and the spread over all six agent signals, the closest analogue here to a deep-ensemble spread, reaches $0.7912$ ($\text{ECE}=0.297$), against $0.8600$ for $U_{\text{SAA}}$ ($\text{ECE}=0.1627$) and $0.8722$ for a single model's own confidence ($\text{ECE}=0.0736$), the standard baseline for recognising misclassified inputs \cite{hendrycks2017baseline}. The ordering cuts both ways: the multi-agent score does outrank both of these baselines out-of-sample, and it is still outranked by the simplest baseline of all, a single model's inverse confidence. This is the same conclusion Section~\ref{sec:signal_decomposition} reaches from a different direction.

Decomposed by true class (Section~\ref{sec:moved_negctrl}), the score ranks errors well among the scene pictures and below chance among the confirmed negatives. The below-chance value is largely arithmetic: on a negative whose object signal is zero an error is impossible, and that is exactly where the derived Agreement term is largest.

\paragraph{A complementary detector.} An error detector built only from raw signals that never enter $SAA$ reaches $\text{ROC-AUC}=0.7299$ but does not separate from confidence (Section~\ref{sec:complementary_detector}).

This also changes what can be said about the complexity-aware triage policy's operating point (Section~\ref{sec:complexity_triage}): re-run on the corrected pool, the SCI-tercile heuristic \emph{still} performs close to chance ($1.15\times$ lift), so that specific negative result was not an artefact of the label contamination -- only the overall generalisation narrative above, and the Conformal Risk Control magnitude of Chapter~\ref{ch:triage_risk_control}, were.


\section{Probabilistic Calibration (Platt Scaling)}
Discrimination and calibration are distinct properties, and modern networks are routinely strong on the first while badly miscalibrated on the second \cite{guo2017calibration}; $\text{ECE}$ is the standard binned estimator of that gap. The deployed fused score is not a probability, so it is mapped to one by \textbf{Platt Scaling} \cite{platt1999probabilistic} (logistic calibration), fitted on a $50\%$ split of $C_1$:
\begin{equation}
    P(y=1 | S_{\text{fusion}}) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp(A \cdot S_{\text{fusion}} + B)}
\end{equation}
On the other half of $C_1$ the calibrated probability reaches an ECE of $0.0044$ ($95\%$ range $[0.0022, 0.0126]$ over $191$ random halvings, $15$ bins), against $0.241$ before calibration. That figure says little about the score: $796$ of the $801$ pictures in $C_1$ are scenes, and always predicting the base rate reaches the same $0.0044$. It is a within-cohort figure, not a general deployment guarantee, as the next paragraph shows.

\paragraph{Calibration at publication level.} That split is a random $50\%$ partition of $C_1$, so pages of one volume fall on both sides and a calibrator profits from having seen sibling scans. The corrected split of Section~\ref{sec:clean_holdout} is PPN-disjoint ($360$ training volumes, $79$ test, none shared). Refitting the same Platt scaling there gives $\text{ECE} = 0.109$ against $0.116$ uncalibrated --- an improvement, but marginal, and more than an order of magnitude from the within-cohort figure. Discrimination is unaffected ($\text{ROC-AUC} = 0.8828$), as expected: a monotone recalibration cannot change ranking, and what fails to transfer across publications is the score-to-probability map, not the ordering. The deployed score therefore \emph{ranks} errors well across publication boundaries and is \emph{not} calibrated across them; an institution relying on the stated probabilities rather than the ranking should recalibrate on its own holdings.



\section{Tier 1 Agent Ablation}
Leave-one-out ablations of the fusion signals and of the four $U_{\text{SAA}}$ terms are reported in Section~\ref{sec:tier1_ablation}. Their main result concerns the Agreement term. Removed from the unweighted spread it costs discrimination ($-0.026$ and $-0.077$ under the two error-label rules); removed from a fitted combination it changes nothing, because on this partition any one of the Object, Agreement and VLM signals can be reconstructed from the other two. $U_{\text{SAA}}$ is nominally a four-signal spread with two independent inputs.

\chapter{Human-in-the-Loop Triage and Conformal Risk Control}
\label{ch:triage_risk_control}

This chapter evaluates the human-in-the-loop (HITL) triage policy and distribution-free risk control for the deployed design. Conformal Risk Control keeps its promise on the corrected, publication-disjoint pool and reduces expert review well below reviewing everything; a simpler SCI-tercile heuristic does not beat random review. The deployed triage score is a disagreement-based score that plain confidence matches (Chapter~\ref{ch:uncertainty_calibration}); the corrected design of Chapter~\ref{ch:corrected_design} applies the same risk control to confidence and approves more.

\section{Complexity-Aware Triage Policy}
\label{sec:complexity_triage}
To account for visual clutter and object density, a first triage policy combined the disagreement proxy with the Spatial Complexity Index ($\text{SCI}_i$). It is the SCI-based variant of Level~4 (Section~\ref{sec:canonical_saa}), not the score the deployed conformal threshold uses:
\begin{equation}
    U^{\text{SCI}}_{\text{triage}, i} = 0.6 \cdot U_{\text{SAA}, i} + 0.4 \cdot (1 - \text{SCI}_i)
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Held-out evaluation.} On the group-level held-out test partition of Section~\ref{sec:clean_holdout}, with SCI terciles fixed in advance from the whole corpus, the SCI-tercile policy routes $47.1\%$ (original pool, $n=401$) and $41.0\%$ (corrected, $n=249$) of the pictures to a person and catches $46.2\%$ and $47.1\%$ of the errors: an efficiency lift of $0.98\times$ and $1.15\times$, close to chance in both cases and far short of conformal risk control on the same pool ($2.31$--$3.09\times$, below). Unlike the first conformal result below, this is not an artefact of the label contamination, and the policy is kept as a negative result.


\section{Distribution-Free Risk Control}
\label{sec:crc_distribution_free}
To provide statistical guarantees without parametric distributional assumptions, \textbf{Conformal Risk Control (CRC)} was applied \cite{crc2024} over $C_1$ ($n=801$, split into calibration set $n_{\text{cal}}=400$ and held-out test set $n_{\text{test}}=401$). The distinction between the two conformal frameworks matters for what is being claimed here. Classical split conformal prediction \cite{vovk2005algorithmic,angelopoulos2024conformal} controls \emph{coverage}: it returns a prediction set guaranteed to contain the true label with probability at least $1-\alpha$, which corresponds to a binary miscoverage loss. Conformal Risk Control \cite{crc2024} generalises this to any monotone, bounded loss function, controlling its \emph{expectation} at $\mathbb{E}[L] \le \alpha$. That generalisation is what this thesis requires, because the quantity to be bounded is not set coverage but the rate at which errors are auto-approved without human audit. Both frameworks assume only exchangeability between calibration and test data; no distributional or parametric assumption about the score is needed.

Let $\alpha = 0.05$ denote the target risk level: on average, at most $5\%$ of all pictures may be both approved automatically and wrong. This is not the error rate among approved pictures, which selective classification bounds instead (see below). The policy approves an image when the system is confident enough in its decision, not merely on the decision's raw score: the classification score $S_{\text{fusion}}$, the stored four-term fusion column (Caution~3, Section~\ref{app:deployed_cautions}), fixes the ground-truth error label, and a separate uncertainty score, $U_{\text{triage}}$, is what determines auto-approval eligibility. The risk functional, matching the deployed implementation exactly, is:
\begin{align}
    \text{error}_i &= \mathbb{I}\left(\mathbb{I}[S_{\text{fusion}, i} \ge 0.5] \neq y_{\text{expert}, i}\right) \\
    U_{\text{triage}, i} &= 0.6 \cdot \sigma_{\text{agents}, i} + 0.4 \cdot (1 - \bar{c}_i) \qquad \text{(Section~\ref{sec:canonical_saa}, Level 4)} \\
    \mathcal{R}(\lambda) &= \mathbb{E}\left[ \text{error}_i \cdot \mathbb{I}\left(U_{\text{triage}, i} < \lambda\right) \right] \le \alpha
\end{align}
Auto-approval occurs when $U_{\text{triage}, i} < \hat\lambda$ (low uncertainty), regardless of what $S_{\text{fusion}}$'s own value was; $S_{\text{fusion}}$ is used only to define $\text{error}_i$, the ground-truth quantity risk is measured against.

Running conformal calibration (Algorithm~\ref{alg:conformal_risk_control}, Section~\ref{sec:crc_algorithm_deployed}) on $n_{\text{cal}}=400$ yields the risk-control threshold $\hat{\lambda} = 0.5370$. Evaluating on the held-out test set ($n_{\text{test}}=401$) demonstrates that:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{R}(\hat{\lambda}) = \mathbf{0.0075} \le \alpha = 0.05 \quad (\text{Conformal Risk Bound Satisfied})
\end{equation}
\textbf{Efficiency lift}, reported alongside every result of this kind, is the share of errors the review queue catches divided by the share of pictures it reviews; random review has a lift of $1$. In this in-sample setting the policy approves most of the collection while keeping the bound (Section~\ref{sec:moved_insample}); the clean evaluation below is the one that counts.

\paragraph{Clean, leak-free evaluation.} The evaluation above shuffles the same $C_1$ cohort already used for weight selection and AWLF development ($99.4\%$ positive), so calibration and test pictures share publications and neither holds a realistic share of negatives. The right test of the guarantee is the identical procedure on a publication-disjoint split of a pool whose labels have been checked.

\paragraph{First attempt: a contaminated pool.} A first group-level split, of the wider $2{,}000$-image pool, appeared to show the guarantee failing (Table~\ref{tab:crc_clean_vs_leaky}). Its negative controls were $66.7\%$ mislabelled (Section~\ref{sec:clean_holdout}); the failure was a property of the labels, not of the procedure.

\section{The Pipeline at Archive Scale}
\label{sec:full_corpus}

Every operating figure reported so far is measured on the $249$-image test partition. The
four Tier~1 models have always been run over the whole archive --- the agent score table
underlying every result in this thesis holds all $12{,}110$ images --- but the \emph{decision}
pipeline, from $SAA$ through $U_{\text{triage}}$ to the conformal threshold, has not been
reported at that scale. Applying the deployed rule ($\hat\lambda = 0.372$ at $\alpha=0.05$)
to all $12{,}110$ answers what an institution would actually experience, and two things it
establishes are not visible on the test partition.

\paragraph{Workload, and a transfer gap.} Across the corpus $8{,}490$ scans clear
automatically and $3{,}620$ reach a person: an auto-approval rate of $70.1\%$ against the
$60.6\%$ measured on the test partition, a gap of $9.5$ percentage points. The direction is
favourable, but it should be read as a caution rather than a bonus: the rate an institution
sees depends on the composition of its holdings, and the test partition is not a scale model
of the archive. Its accuracy was checked afterwards on a random sample of $247$ of these automatic approvals, labelled by the author under the scene rule of Section~\ref{sec:archive_check}: $52$ of them ($21.1\%$, $95\%$ interval $[16.4\%, 26.6\%]$) show no scene. Part of that is the difference between the two definitions of a scene that Section~\ref{sec:archive_check} describes.

\paragraph{Three checks at archive scale.} Section~\ref{sec:archive_queue} adds three checks: in the deployed signals' eight cells the labelled pool is close to the corpus (total variation distance $0.076$), although Section~\ref{sec:archive_check} shows that it is not representative in its labels, which is what decides the risk; $61.2\%$ of the pictures sent to a person come from a single combination of signal values; and the threshold cannot be tuned to reduce that workload, because it sits just below that combination's triage score and does not move under bootstrap.

\chapter{Downstream Curation, Retrieval, and Cross-Archive Utility}
\label{ch:downstream_utility}

This chapter evaluates the downstream curation and retrieval agents of Tier~2, which work on the metadata the Tier~1 agents produce and never change the Tier~1 decision.

\section{The Contradiction Checker}
\label{sec:agent2_contradiction}
The contradiction checker flags candidate hallucinations by computing set-differences between VLM-mentioned nouns and spatial object bounding boxes: any noun the VLM reports with no corresponding YOLO detection is flagged as a candidate contradiction. It runs on every image: the VLM's free-text answers are mapped to the detector's classes by a word list, and with the deployed detector $72.1\%$ of the archive's pictures receive at least one flag.

Vision-language models are known to name objects that are not in the picture, most often objects that are common in their training data or that usually occur together with what is shown \cite{li2023pope}; a contradiction check of this kind targets exactly that failure.

\paragraph{A precision figure from the first prototype.} Every candidate contradiction across the $25$ images where an early prototype's scene-graph and detection outputs disagree ($127$ candidates total; the prototype covered $50$ images and used an earlier detector with general object classes) was labelled by the author as \textit{present} (a YOLO miss, not a hallucination), \textit{absent} (a hallucination), or \textit{ambiguous}. Of the $127$ candidates, $46$ ($39.7\%$ of the $116$ unambiguous cases) are hallucinations, $70$ are YOLO under-detecting something the VLM correctly saw, and $11$ are ambiguous. Twenty-four of the $46$ come from a single emblem, to which the VLM attached a list of virtues (honesty, loyalty, perseverance and the like) and heraldic terms; without those, $22$ of $92$ ($23.9\%$) are hallucinations. The checker's own framing as a ``hallucination detector'' is therefore precise for at most two in five of what it flags, and for about one in four outside that one picture -- ``a cross-modal disagreement worth review'' is the more accurate description, and the model most often at fault when that disagreement fires is YOLO, not the VLM.

\paragraph{The agent as it runs now, on the 300 annotated pictures.} The prototype figure does not describe the agent that runs on the archive, which compares the VLM's answers with the archival detector. That agent was scored, with the evaluation fixed in writing before it was run, on the $300$ pictures annotated with the eight object classes: a flag on a class the annotation does not show counts as a hallucination, a flag on a class it shows as a detector miss. With the deployed detector, the checker raises $233$ flags on $171$ of the $300$ pictures, and $56.2\%$ of them name a class the annotation does not show ($95\%$ interval $[49.8\%, 62.4\%]$), against $36.5\%$ of all the classes the VLM names; it flags $89.7\%$ of those unsupported claims. With the retrained object finder, which misses fewer objects, it raises $125$ flags, $68.0\%$ of them unsupported ($[59.4\%, 75.5\%]$), and catches $58.2\%$ of the unsupported claims. A flag therefore carries real information, more with the better detector, but a third or more of the flags are still detector misses. Two things make these shares upper bounds for genuine hallucinations: an object the annotation left out, such as a small background tree, counts as a hallucination, and in $20$ of the $146$ unsupported claims the VLM only hedged (``possibly a bridge'').


\section{The Cross-Archive Linker}
\label{sec:agent4_linking}
The cross-archive linker was designed to embed each image's VLM scene description and find nearest neighbours across different publication series (PPNs), surfacing candidate recurring compositions or motifs. No implementation of that text-based version survives in the project's code, and none was evaluated; the version that runs on the archive and is evaluated below compares SigLIP image embeddings instead. The text descriptions would also have been poor material: $8$ records ($0.07\%$) store an HTTP time-out message in place of a response, and $2{,}325$ ($19.2\%$) contain injected Chinese-character tokens inside German or English output, so that in a text-similarity proxy several cross-volume pairs match perfectly on a shared error string rather than on a shared picture.

\paragraph{A visual-embedding benchmark.} Beyond the text-based proxy above, a dedicated benchmark implements the linker with image embeddings: SigLIP image embeddings (the same model family already deployed as the scene-classification agent) for a sample of $400$ accessible images, with cosine similarity ranked over cross-PPN pairs only. Visually checking the top matches against the source images gives two concrete findings. \emph{(1) A true match}: the single highest-similarity pair ($0.938$) is the literal same illustration reprinted across two different digitised volumes (\texttt{PPN1852785039\_00000183\_0.jpg} and \texttt{PPN1885972776\_00000183\_0.jpg}; both on page 183, so possibly the same book digitised twice) -- identical figures and composition, confirming the mechanism finds real recurring compositions when they exist. \emph{(2) A real limitation}: the next-highest pair ($0.901$) is visually confirmed to be two \emph{different} compositions -- a court scene and a street scene -- that share only engraving style and period costume, not the same picture. Raw visual-embedding similarity in the $0.90$ band captures illustration style/genre, not compositional recurrence specifically; a deployed system using a single global similarity threshold would need either a higher threshold or an additional discriminating signal to separate true reprints from same-genre false positives.

\paragraph{A gold-labelled precision-at-threshold curve.} The two-pair spot-check above motivated a larger test: SigLIP embeddings were computed over $1{,}500$ corpus images (same method as above) and the $80$ highest-similarity cross-archive pairs kept (similarity $0.873$--$0.985$), all computed, not hand-picked. The author judged each of the $79$ labelled pairs as the same composition (a real reprint or duplicate), the same style only (the style-versus-composition confound above), or unrelated. Overall, only $55.7\%$ ($44$ of $79$) are true matches; $38.0\%$ ($30$) are style-only false positives, and $5$ are unrelated. Precision rises monotonically with similarity -- $55.7\%$ at $\ge 0.87$, $91.3\%$ at $\ge 0.90$ -- and reaches a clean, stable $100\%$ plateau at every threshold $\ge 0.92$ tested (down to $n=1$ at $\ge 0.98$). This gives an evidence-based recommendation in place of a guess: a deployed system should threshold the linker's cross-archive candidates at similarity $\ge 0.92$ (still retaining $41$ of the $79$ top candidates) for high precision ($41$ of $41$ true, a $95\%$ interval reaching down to about $91\%$); below that band almost all candidates are same-style or unrelated ($3$ true matches among $38$ pairs).

\paragraph{Across the whole archive.} Run over all $12{,}110$ pictures with SigLIP image embeddings and neighbours restricted to a different volume, $2{,}434$ pictures ($20.1\%$) have a cross-volume neighbour at similarity $0.92$ or above, the level at which every sampled pair was a true match. These are candidate links for a person to confirm. Three things are known about them. $265$ of the $2{,}434$ links ($10.9\%$) join pictures with the same page number in both volumes, most likely the same book digitised twice; among the labelled pairs, $10$ of the $44$ true matches were of this kind and $34$ were reprints at a different place. And the $100\%$ precision at $0.92$ was measured on the highest-scoring pairs of a $1{,}500$-picture sample; across $12{,}110$ pictures each picture has many more candidates, so a same-style neighbour reaches $0.92$ more easily. A test fixed in advance therefore measured it: of $50$ links drawn at random from the $2{,}434$ and judged blind to their similarity, $38$ show the same picture, a precision of $76.0\%$ ($95\%$ interval $[62.6\%, 85.7\%]$), and the other $12$ only a picture in the same style; none is unrelated. All $5$ links between equal page numbers were the same picture, against $33$ of the $45$ others ($73.3\%$, $[59.0\%, 84.0\%]$). In a breakdown added after the planned test, the same-style links sit at the low end of the range: $9$ of the $17$ links below a similarity of $0.94$ were same style only, against $3$ of the $33$ above it. About three in four archive-wide links are thus real reprints, and a person confirming them is best served by starting at the highest similarities.

\section{The Inscription Reader}
\label{sec:agent5_ocr}
The inscription reader uses Kosmos-2.5 to extract bounding-box-aligned textual inscriptions from historical blackboards and catalogue inscriptions. It was meant to corroborate scene labels; in the corrected design it takes no part in the decision (Table~\ref{tab:what_changed}). This mechanism is implemented and runs on every image. No quantitative benchmark against ungrounded OCR exists (that remains future work), but three checks -- mechanical and corpus-wide, visual on a small sample, and a gold-labelled precision/WER benchmark -- surface a previously undocumented failure mode worth disclosing.

\paragraph{A corpus-wide three-bucket benchmark.} Autoregressive text models can fail in at least two mechanically-detectable ways: looping (repeating a token or short phrase until the length limit), and hallucinated fluent captioning (describing generic image content instead of reading text). Classifying all $12{,}110$ deployed Kosmos-2.5 outputs by these two signals finds \textbf{$49.8\%$ degenerate repetition} (unique/total word ratio $<0.3$), a \textbf{lower-bound $3.8\%$ hallucinated captioning} (matching common caption-opener phrasing, e.g.\ ``a picture of\ldots''), and a \textbf{remaining $46.4\%$} not mechanically distinguishable from real but degraded transcription without visual inspection. These figures describe the deployed outputs; on the outputs of the full rerun the same rule gives $49.5\%$, $3.1\%$ and $47.3\%$. The third bucket's exact composition is tested by the visual sampling below.

\paragraph{A visual spot-check.} Comparing a handful of outputs with their source images shows the three behaviours behind the buckets: a magic-lantern slide with legible printed text whose output is the single token ``the'' repeated to the length limit; a page of Fraktur script read as badly garbled but recognisably real text; and fluent caption prose (``The image above is a sample of a text file\ldots'') that reads no text at all.

\paragraph{A gold-labelled bucket-precision and word-error-rate benchmark.} The author transcribed the actual text (or confirmed \texttt{NO\_TEXT}) for $53$ images sampled across all three buckets ($40$ from ``candidate real OCR,'' $8$ from ``degenerate repetition,'' $5$ from ``hallucinated captioning''), directly testing whether each mechanical bucket label means what it claims. The classifier is validated on only one of its three buckets. \textit{Hallucinated captioning} holds up perfectly ($5/5=100\%$ precision as a ``no usable text'' detector). \textit{Degenerate repetition} is only $50\%$ precise ($4$ of $8$): the other half of these images do have real legible text that Kosmos-2.5 simply failed to extract, a previously undocumented false-negative mode the repetition heuristic cannot see. \textit{Candidate real OCR} -- the largest bucket, and the one this thesis's $46.4\%$ headline figure describes as ``candidate real (if often degraded) OCR'' -- is only $52.5\%$ precise ($21$ of $40$) as a detector that real text is even present: for $47.5\%$ ($19$ of $40$) of these images there is no text at all in the source image, and Kosmos-2.5's fluent-looking markdown output (tables, prices, repeated image captions, structured headers) is pure fabrication with no basis in the image, not degraded OCR. Even restricted to the $21$ images that do have real text, transcription quality remains poor: a word-error-rate calculation (Levenshtein word-edit distance against the human transcription) gives a median WER of $100\%$ -- as many word-level corrections are needed as there are words in the correct answer -- driven mostly by non-transcription markdown and structural hallucination mixed into or substituted for actual reading. None of the inscription reader's three buckets can be trusted as a precision signal without human review, and ``candidate real OCR'' in particular is closer to a coin flip on whether real text is even present than a positive indicator.

\section{A Retrieval Pilot}
\label{sec:retrieval_benchmark}
Ten archival research queries on the Colibri archive ($C_0$, $n=12{,}110$) compare the semantic index over the system's metadata with keyword search over captions, by Precision@10 (the share of relevant pictures among the first ten). Two kinds of relevance label exist.

\paragraph{Proxy labels.} Against proxy labels (scene-label match plus an agent-agreement threshold), computed partly from the same agent scores the semantic index uses, the index reaches Precision@10 $=1.000$ against $0.180$ for keyword search and is ahead on all $10$ queries. Because the proxy shares inputs with the index, this figure is circular: it shows that the pipeline runs, not how well it retrieves.

\paragraph{Judged by a person (planned; run on part of the pictures).} A test fixed in advance (Section~\ref{sec:planned_analyses}) had the author judge the $196$ retrieved pictures, shuffled within each query and blind to the method, score and proxy label. Judging stopped at $94$ pictures; the queries were judged in order, so the first $4$ are complete and the other $6$ are left out. On those $4$, Precision@10 judged by the person is $0.60$ for the semantic index and $0.10$ for keyword search ($0.62$ and $0.11$ with the unsure answers left out), and the index is ahead on all $4$: $0.8$, $0.1$, $0.7$ and $0.8$ for ``children playing outdoors'', ``classroom teaching scene'', ``family portrait domestic'' and ``landscape nature outdoor''. The proxy agrees with the person on $71.3\%$ of the $94$ judged pictures ($\kappa = 0.43$); where they differ, it mostly calls a picture relevant that the person does not ($19$ against $8$). The proxy thus overstates the index's precision ($1.000$ against $0.60$ on the same queries), while the order of the two methods holds. Four queries taken in order rather than at random, and one judge, make this a pilot.

A small-scale ($n=24$) cross-institutional pilot on the real Europeana Open Culture API further probes generalisation beyond this thesis's primary archive; see Appendix~\ref{app:europeana} for the full pilot and what is and is not yet established by it.

\chapter{Conclusions, Ethical Considerations, and Future Work}
\label{ch:conclusions}

\section{Summary of Thesis Contributions}
This thesis makes seven contributions. The first two concern how a detector for this archive is trained and graded, the third the main research question, the fourth and fifth the corrected design that follows from its answer, on the class tasks and on the archive, and the last two risk control and downstream use.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Pseudo-label collapse warning.} Uncovered the proxy-to-human mAP collapse ($0.768 \rightarrow 0.146$), demonstrating that grading a student against its teacher's labels hides what the teacher never labelled (Section~\ref{sec:collapse_paradox}).
    \item \textbf{The teacher, not the training.} Traced the collapse to the teacher: of 128 objects the student missed on pictures the teacher had labelled, 126 were never in the teacher's labels (the count covers missed objects, not misplaced or false boxes). A new open-vocabulary teacher raises the student from a mean AP50 of $0.123$ to $0.210$ against human boxes (Section~\ref{sec:student_rebuild}), and a controlled test shows that filtering the teacher's labels by agreement with a second model has no effect it can establish ($0.214$, $0.232$ and $0.201$ for all, confirmed and randomly thinned labels; an earlier run with the same seeds ordered them the other way; the student reported in Section~\ref{sec:student_rebuild} is the first run of the confirmed set; Section~\ref{sec:label_filter}).
    \item \textbf{The deployed uncertainty score, and what it actually measures.} The deployed uncertainty proxy $U_{\text{SAA}}$ ranks errors at $\text{ROC-AUC}=0.9791$ on $C_1$ ($n=801$), but its performance is carried by a term algebraically derived from two of its own inputs; a plain confidence baseline matches it in-sample and slightly beats it out of sample, and the error label it is evaluated on is partly circular (Section~\ref{sec:signal_decomposition}). A pre-registered test on the corrected design's agents, with a follow-up within each task, gives the same answer at the level of single classes (Section~\ref{sec:c4_preregistered}).
    \item \textbf{A corrected design, evaluated on the scene and object classes.} Replaced the deployed signals, which reduce to two measurements and eight states, by three agents that each report what they measure, mapped to probabilities and routed by confidence (Chapter~\ref{ch:corrected_design}). For the eight object classes the agents rank pictures containing a class above those without it at a mean AUROC of $0.903$, and a promise on missed objects gives each picture a list of $2.8$ classes that leaves out $9.2\%$ of a picture's objects on average at $\alpha = 0.10$, as promised ($10.8\%$ of all objects present); single objects are not confirmed automatically, because a promise on all decisions is kept by ``absent'' calls (Section~\ref{sec:objects_llava_vs_fusion}). For the five scene classes the agents rank and shortlist but do not decide. Their combination must be learned: one joint mapping of all the agents' reports raises the balanced accuracy from $0.401$ with the inherited fixed weights to $0.544$, and finds family, playing and landscape in $27$--$62\%$ of cases ($55$--$86\%$ with class-balanced fitting, an analysis added after the plan). Under a $5\%$ promise ``no scene'' is approved reliably and ``drawing'' with about one answer in six wrong; the specific classes need a person (Section~\ref{sec:scene_classes}). On part of the planned random archive pictures the scenes are mostly playing rather than drawing, and the mapping learned on the pool reaches a balanced accuracy of $0.351$ (Section~\ref{sec:archive_check}). The vision-language model alone is about as good as the learned combination.
    \item \textbf{Scene presence, and a risk promise tested on the archive.} At the first level of the scene decision, whether a picture shows a scene, the corrected design approves $13.5$ points more at $\alpha=0.05$ and $26.1$ more at $\alpha=0.10$ than the deployed design's triage score over 200 book-disjoint redraws ($15.3$ and $27.7$ more than routing by its disagreement alone), on at least 199 of 200 redraws, and more at the same realised risk from about $0.05$ upward (Section~\ref{sec:matched_realised_risk}); every approved picture in the pool is a confident scene call, and the combination adds about two points of approval over the describer alone, which is not established. On $400$ pictures drawn at random from the archive the promise fails when the cut-off is calibrated on the labelled pool ($16.2\%$ approved and wrong at $\alpha=0.05$); learning under one definition of a scene and calibrating on archive pictures brings it to just above the promised level at about $80\%$ approved ($0.054$--$0.059$ on $294$ new pictures in a test fixed in advance). Calibrated at $\alpha=0.025$ it gave $0.046$ with $64\%$ approved, below $5\%$ but not within the $2.5\%$ promised; the two random samples differ more than chance makes likely, which points to a change in the labels between sessions (Section~\ref{sec:archive_check}).
    \item \textbf{Conformal risk control, and a data-integrity correction along the way.} Conformal risk control \cite{crc2024} keeps its bound on the corrected labelled pool at every $\alpha$ tested: at $\hat{\lambda}=0.372$ the empirical risk is $\mathcal{R}(\hat{\lambda})=0.0201 \le 0.05$, approving $60.6\%$ of pictures automatically at $91.1\%$ error recall ($2.31\times$--$3.09\times$ lift). An earlier clean re-evaluation appeared to show the guarantee failing ($\mathcal{R}(\hat\lambda)=0.182$); the cause was an evaluation pool whose negative controls were mostly mislabelled, found and corrected in two labelling passes by the author (Section~\ref{sec:clean_holdout}). The simpler SCI-tercile heuristic performs close to chance on every version of the pool (Section~\ref{sec:complexity_triage}).
    \item \textbf{Downstream archival utility.} In a retrieval pilot judged by a person on $4$ queries, the semantic index reaches Precision@10 $=0.60$ against $0.10$ for keyword search; proxy labels that share its inputs overstate it as $1.000$. A cross-institutional pilot ($C_5$, $24$ Europeana images) is a negative transfer result: the unchanged pipeline misses $65.2\%$ of confirmed scenes (Appendix~\ref{app:europeana}).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{What the multi-agent design contributes.} The title promises a multi-agent system for object and complex scene detection, and the thesis builds and tests one; what it finds the separate agents are good for differs from what they were built for. They do not make the decisions better: one vision-language model alone is about as good as the combination on both class tasks and at the first level of the scene decision (Sections~\ref{sec:scene_classes}, \ref{sec:objects_llava_vs_fusion} and~\ref{sec:corrected_results}), and their disagreement warns of errors no better than confidence (Section~\ref{sec:c4_preregistered}). They contribute in three other ways. First, they made the central question testable: only with reports from separate sources could the thesis measure whether disagreement signals doubt, and show that most errors lie where the agents agree (Section~\ref{sec:blind_spot_features}). Second, each does something the others cannot: the object finder alone says where an object is, the scene matcher can be asked about any class in one sentence and can say that none fits, and the describer gives a graded answer for every class. Third, a learned combination of their reports gives the probabilities a risk promise needs, and for the scene classes it is far better than fixed weights (a balanced accuracy of $0.544$ against $0.401$). A cataloguing system built today could therefore start from one vision-language model under a risk promise calibrated on its own collection, and add agents for what that model cannot do, such as locating objects.

\paragraph{What can be used today.} On this archive the following are ready for use, each with the person stated: the scene-presence decision, after calibrating on a random sample of the collection labelled under the definition the archive means to apply (Section~\ref{sec:archive_check}); a short list of object classes per picture with a stated share of missed objects, which a person confirms (Section~\ref{sec:objects_llava_vs_fusion}); the scene classes as a ranked shortlist for a person, not as decisions; and the Tier~2 agents as aids to a curator, not as sources of catalogue entries. Not ready: boxes for the rare object classes, automatic confirmation of single objects, the inscription reader's transcriptions, and any risk promise on a collection without its own calibration sample.

\section{Revisiting the Research Questions}
Section~\ref{sec:objectives} posed four research questions. Each is answered here with the same calibration applied throughout this thesis: a scoped ``yes'' where the evidence supports one, an explicit ``partially'' where it supports only part, and a plain ``no'' where it goes against the question, rather than a uniform claim of success.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1 (Uncertainty from Disagreement) -- No}: On this archive, disagreement between the agents does not warn of the system's errors better than the system's own confidence. In a test fixed before it was run, per-class disagreement ranks errors at $0.378$ against $0.825$ for plain confidence on the 300 hand-annotated pictures (difference $-0.448$, $[-0.541, -0.354]$; Section~\ref{sec:c4_preregistered}). That signal is computed on the object classes and the errors on the scene call; measured within each task in a follow-up, disagreement ranks errors at $0.781$ for object labels and $0.815$ for scene presence, against $0.835$ and $0.825$ for confidence, so it carries information but not more than confidence. Agreement between models also fails as a filter for training labels (Section~\ref{sec:label_filter}). The deployed proxy's high figure ($\text{ROC-AUC}=0.9791$ on $C_1$) is carried by a derived term: without it the in-sample ROC-AUC drops to $0.7466$, while a plain confidence score with no disagreement information scores $0.9780$ (Section~\ref{sec:signal_decomposition}). On confirmed true negatives disagreement even ranks errors below chance, largely for an arithmetic reason (Section~\ref{sec:clean_holdout}). In both class tasks the vision-language model alone is about as good as the combination of agents (Sections~\ref{sec:scene_classes} and~\ref{sec:objects_llava_vs_fusion}).
    \item \textbf{RQ2 (Workload Reduction) -- Yes, with a condition}: The corrected design approves $76.4\%$ of pictures automatically at $\alpha = 0.05$ against $63.0\%$ for the deployed design's triage score ($61.1\%$ when it routes by disagreement alone), and $89.1\%$ against $63.0\%$ ($61.5\%$) at $\alpha=0.10$, over 200 book-disjoint redraws (Chapter~\ref{ch:corrected_design}). The guarantee is met on average over calibration sets and not on every batch, and the larger approval uses the whole risk budget, which the deployed design could not. These figures hold inside the labelled pool. On pictures drawn at random from the archive a cut-off calibrated on the pool does not keep the promise; one definition of a scene and calibration on a random sample of the archive bring it to about the promised level at about four pictures in five approved, just above $0.05$ on new pictures; calibrated at $\alpha = 0.025$ it stays below $0.05$ ($0.046$, about two in three approved) but not within the $2.5\%$ promised (Section~\ref{sec:archive_check}). The answer is therefore yes, on the condition that the calibration pictures come from the collection the system is applied to; a stricter level gives a margin, not a guarantee. For the class tasks the reduction takes another form. For the eight object classes a person confirms a short list instead of searching each picture for eight classes: at $\alpha = 0.10$ the list holds $2.8$ classes and leaves out $10.8\%$ of the objects present (Section~\ref{sec:objects_llava_vs_fusion}). For the five scene classes the promise approves ``no scene'' reliably and ``drawing'' with about one answer in six wrong, and the specific classes go to a person (Section~\ref{sec:scene_classes}).
    \item \textbf{RQ3 (Contradiction Detection) -- Partially}: The contradiction checker is implemented and runs on every image. On the first prototype's $127$ candidates $39.7\%$ of the unambiguous flags were hallucinations ($23.9\%$ without $24$ words attached to one emblem); scored on the $300$ annotated pictures, the agent as it runs now flags a class the annotation does not show in $56.2\%$ of its flags with the deployed detector and $68.0\%$ with the retrained object finder, against $36.5\%$ of all the VLM's claims. Its flags carry information, but a third or more are objects the detector missed. By design it does not feed back into the decision, so the question as posed, whether it improves the decision, is not answered; what is established is how often its flags are right.
    \item \textbf{RQ4 (Retrieval \& Cross-Archive Discovery) -- Partially}: In a retrieval pilot judged by a person, the semantic index finds $6$ relevant pictures in ten against $1$ for keyword search, on $4$ queries; the proxy labels used before overstate it. Transfer to other institutions does not hold: applied unchanged to $23$ human-labelled Europeana images, the pipeline misses $65.2\%$ of confirmed scenes, and there its agents span only about one independent direction (effective rank $1.37$, against $2.50$ on Colibri; Section~\ref{sec:heterogeneity_precondition}). Within the archive, the cross-archive linker links $2{,}434$ pictures to a picture in another volume at a similarity of $0.92$ or above, where every checked pair of a sample was a reprint; at that scale $38$ of $50$ randomly drawn links ($76\%$) show the same picture and the rest a picture in the same style (Section~\ref{sec:agent4_linking}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Ethical Considerations}
\label{sec:ethics}
Three ethical questions arise from this work, and they are separable.

\paragraph{Synthetic detail must not become evidence.} Generative restoration (Real-ESRGAN for the whole corpus; CycleGAN and Stable Diffusion as well on a subset of $231$ scans) invents texture that was never in the original scan. That is acceptable as a way of moving an image closer to the distribution a detector was trained on; it is not acceptable as a historical record. This thesis therefore enforces a strict boundary: restored imagery ($\mathbf{I}_{\text{refined}}$) is an internal representation used only for model inference, never primary evidence, and every piece of catalogue metadata anchors back to the original digitised scan ($\mathbf{I}_{\text{raw}}$). The distinction matters because a restored image is persuasive in a way a degraded one is not, and a catalogue that stored the restored version would be quietly substituting a model's reconstruction for the artefact.

\paragraph{Automated description imposes present-day categories on historical material.} The taxonomy this system applies was fixed in the present and the models behind it learned their visual semantics from contemporary web imagery. Applied to a historical archive, that risks describing historical content in terms its creators would not recognise, and doing so at a scale and with an apparent authority that no individual cataloguer would claim. Section~\ref{sec:annotation_ambiguity_note} documents categories that are contested among specialists rather than merely difficult; the appropriate response is to treat every automated description as a proposition to be checked, which is what the escalation policy is for, and to keep the model's output distinguishable from a curator's judgement in the record.

\paragraph{The risk level is an institutional choice, not a technical one.} Conformal Risk Control bounds the rate of errors approved without review, but only once someone has chosen $\alpha$. That choice decides how many incorrect descriptions enter a public catalogue unseen, and it is a question about an institution's obligations to its users rather than one this thesis can answer. Two properties of the system bear on it. The bound holds only relative to the distribution it was calibrated on, so an institution adopting it inherits the obligation to recalibrate rather than assume transfer; Section~\ref{sec:archive_check} shows the bound failing on this very archive when it is calibrated on a pool that was not drawn from it. And the errors that pass unreviewed are not a random sample of errors: Section~\ref{sec:blind_spot_features} shows they concentrate where the models agree confidently and are wrong together, which is precisely where no automated signal will flag them.

\section{What This Thesis Does NOT Claim (Boundaries of Scientific Validity)}
\label{sec:not_claim}
Each boundary below is argued in full where it arises; they are collected here so that later work does not inherit a claim this evidence cannot carry.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Disagreement is not shown to beat confidence.} The deployed $U_{\text{SAA}}$ does not measure epistemic breakdown directly, and a single model's own confidence ties it ($0.9767$ against $0.9791$ on $C_1$). The claim was retested on signals rebuilt from the raw model outputs and against a label from human review (Section~\ref{sec:signal_rebuild}), and across nine formulations of the score (Section~\ref{sec:formulation_search}); none beat plain confidence. Three further limits apply to the deployed figures: the ensemble carried three measured signals, not four, because two were read from one vision-language answer (Section~\ref{sec:canonical_saa}); its error label shares the score's inputs, and scored by rebuilt signals that do not feed the label, the same task falls from $\text{ROC-AUC}=0.8799$ to $0.6225$ (Section~\ref{sec:signal_rebuild}); and the below-chance result on confirmed true negatives is largely arithmetic, not evidence of correlated errors (Section~\ref{sec:clean_holdout}). What is defensible is risk-controlled routing by confidence, as in the corrected design.
    \item \textbf{The transfer and retrieval pilots are pilots.} The cross-institutional pilot does not show transfer; at the classification level it shows the opposite, missing $65.2\%$ of confirmed scenes, and its error-detection half is unanswerable because the ensemble collapsed there to a single varying agent (Appendix~\ref{app:europeana}). The retrieval result judged by a person covers $4$ of $10$ queries and one judge (Section~\ref{sec:retrieval_benchmark}).
    \item \textbf{The conformal guarantee is relative to its calibration distribution.} A distribution-free bound is only as trustworthy as the match between its calibration set and the pictures it is applied to (Sections~\ref{sec:risk_promise_explained} and~\ref{sec:archive_check}), and on this archive the match fails: calibrated on the labelled pool, the corrected design lets $16.2\%$ of randomly drawn archive pictures through approved and wrong at $\alpha=0.05$. Calibrated on archive pictures it comes close ($0.054$--$0.059$ on new pictures at $\alpha=0.05$, and $0.046$ at $\alpha=0.025$, which is not within the $2.5\%$ promised; Section~\ref{sec:archive_check}).
    \item \textbf{The corrected design does not decide scene / no scene better.} At its line of $0.5$ it calls almost every picture a scene, and with a fitted line its balanced accuracy is not clearly better than the deployed design's ($0.815$ against $0.798$; Section~\ref{sec:decision_line}). Its gain is in how many confident scene calls can be approved under the promise, not in the decision itself. With the pool's mixed labels it cannot label pictures ``no scene'' automatically: with a fitted line and a separate promise for each answer, about half of the ``no scene'' calls it approves are wrong (Section~\ref{sec:decision_line}). Under one definition of a scene this changes: on random archive pictures $80$ of its $112$ ``no scene'' calls are approved and one of them is wrong (Section~\ref{sec:archive_check}).
    \item \textbf{The risk promise is an average, not a per-batch guarantee.} Conformal risk control bounds the expected share of wrong automatic approvals over calibration sets. For the corrected design that average is met without margin ($0.0498$ at $\alpha=0.05$), and a single split exceeds the level in $41.5\%$ of cases; calibrating at $\alpha=0.025$ keeps nine batches in ten at or below $0.05$ while still approving $67.2\%$ (Section~\ref{sec:matched_realised_risk}).
    \item \textbf{Objects are not confirmed automatically.} Under a risk promise per class, at most $0.4\%$ of the objects present are confirmed without a person, and for rare classes most of them are approved as absent: the promise on the share of all decisions is kept by the absent calls. A promise on missed objects is kept instead, but it delivers a list from which a person strikes out the wrong classes ($1.56$ per picture at $\alpha=0.10$; Section~\ref{sec:objects_llava_vs_fusion}).
    \item \textbf{Specific scene classes are not assigned automatically.} Under a risk promise the corrected design approves ``no scene'' reliably and ``drawing'' with about one answer in six wrong; the few landscape and family answers it approves are wrong $16$--$30\%$ of the time, so the specific classes need a person. The short lists contain the right class far less often for the rare classes than on average, unless the classes are weighted equally, and the classes rest on one expert's labels, on which people agree only moderately (Section~\ref{sec:scene_classes}).
    \item \textbf{Restoration is not shown to help.} For the zero-shot scene agent the original scans do as well as the restored ones (Section~\ref{sec:restoration_ablation}); the other agents were not re-scored.
    \item \textbf{Several agents are not shown to be better than one.} In both class tasks the vision-language model alone is about as good as the combination of the three agents; a learned combination is slightly ahead on the scene classes without an established margin, and at the first level of the scene decision the combination's advantage over it is not established (Sections~\ref{sec:scene_classes}, \ref{sec:objects_llava_vs_fusion} and~\ref{sec:corrected_results}). A linear probe on one model's image embedding is also level with the system on both scene tasks, though not on objects (Section~\ref{sec:against_baselines}). The value of the separate agents in this thesis is diagnostic: they made it possible to test the disagreement hypothesis and to see where the errors lie.
    \item \textbf{Agreement between models does not pick better training labels.} Filtering the teacher's labels by a second model's agreement leaves the student's accuracy unchanged (Section~\ref{sec:label_filter}); the improved object finder is due to the better teacher alone.
    \item \textbf{``Scene'' at the first level mixed two definitions.} In the expert labels the scene / no-scene target counts any illustration labelled ``drawing'' as a scene, so for those pictures it is closer to ``illustration or not'' than to the presence of a scene, while the 2026 relabelling rounds use the narrow sense. Section~\ref{sec:archive_check} fixes one definition and repeats the test under it; that relabelling rests on one person's labels. The corrected design also finds scenes in the narrow sense (Section~\ref{sec:scene_definition}), but that result rests on one expert's scene types.
    \item \textbf{The Tier 2 curation agents are aids, not autonomous judgements.} All three now carry gold-labelled precision figures, and none is reliable enough to stand alone: the contradiction checker $39.7\%$ on the first prototype ($56.2\%$ as it runs now, $68.0\%$ with the retrained object finder), the cross-archive linker $55.7\%$ on the top pairs of a sample and $76.0\%$ on $50$ random archive-wide links above $0.92$, and the inscription reader $52.5\%$ (Chapter~\ref{ch:downstream_utility}). Per the Architectural Decoupling Rule none of them feeds $U_{\text{SAA}}$, the triage results, or any headline claim.
\end{itemize}

Of these, the rows of low annotation effort are the ones a continuation of this work should take first, above all a second rater on the random archive pictures: they are bounded effort, and they tighten estimates this thesis already reports as provisional.

\paragraph{Why the Tier 2 curation agents are not ablated.} An ablation measures a component's contribution to an outcome, and by the Architectural Decoupling Rule (Section~\ref{sec:tiers}) the Tier 2 agents have no causal path to any outcome reported here: they consume post-validation metadata and feed neither $U_{\text{SAA}}$, the triage score, nor the conformal policy. Ablating them would move nothing, and reporting that as a finding would present an architectural fact as an empirical one. The right instrument for a downstream curation aid is its precision when a human acts on it, which is what Chapter~\ref{ch:downstream_utility} reports. The one place an ablation would inform is retrieval, whose ranking does consume Tier 2 output; that evaluation is a pilot judged by a person on four queries (Section~\ref{sec:retrieval_benchmark}), too small to support one, and enlarging it is listed in Table~\ref{tab:future_work}.

\textbf{Audit the provenance of an evaluation pool}: the label-integrity failure of Section~\ref{sec:clean_holdout} was found by auditing where the pool's labels came from, not by checking that its results reproduced. The standing lesson is to audit an evaluation pool's provenance, and more than once, before trusting any negative result derived from it.

\textbf{A designed inter-annotator study}: the agreement figures of this thesis ($\kappa=0.337$ between the expert and the author, $0.36$ for the author with themselves) come from passes designed for other purposes. A double annotation with both raters blind to each other, on a sample that includes true negatives, would measure it properly.

\textbf{A better list of scene classes}: the five classes were fixed at the outset without written definitions, and ``drawing'' collects most of the scenes. Section~\ref{sec:drawing_clusters} shows that it hides at least four recurring subjects, among them religious scenes, animals, and riders and battles. A successor should define the classes in a written codebook from the start, ideally by mapping them to Iconclass, the standard subject classification for images in art and cultural heritage \cite{iconclass}; organise them in two levels, a broad subject (people, animals, landscape, religion) and an activity within it; let a picture carry more than one class, since a family can be shown at play; and measure agreement between two people on the codebook before labelling at scale. The design needs no retraining for this: the scene matcher and the describer are zero-shot, so a new class needs one sentence and one question, and only the small learned mapping needs labelled examples of it.

\textbf{Other domains}: the core mechanism, a model's confidence thresholded under a risk promise calibrated on the collection itself, does not depend on anything specific to historical scans and could serve other settings with strong domain shift, such as medical, satellite or archaeological imagery. That is a conjecture, not a result; the collapse of the ensemble on a second collection of the same kind (Section~\ref{sec:heterogeneity_precondition}) is a reason for caution.

\section{Level 1: the signal constructions and their reachable values}

For an image $i$ the Tier 1 stage produces four scalars in $[0,1]$:
\begin{align}
A_i^{\text{object}} &= \min\!\left(\tfrac{k_i}{3},\, 1\right), \qquad k_i = \text{VQA object count} \label{eq:d-obj}\\
A_i^{\text{scene}}  &= \operatorname{clip}_{[0,1]}\!\left(c_i^{\text{scene}}\right), \qquad c_i^{\text{scene}} = \text{VQA scene confidence} \label{eq:d-scene}\\
A_i^{\text{vlm}}    &= \text{VQA object-presence score} \in \{0,1\} \label{eq:d-vlm}\\
A_i^{\text{ocr}}    &= \min\!\left(\tfrac{\ell_i}{80},\, 1\right), \qquad \ell_i = \text{characters of OCR output} \label{eq:d-ocr}
\end{align}

Equation~\ref{eq:d-obj} saturates at $k_i=3$, so it can only return
\[
A^{\text{object}} \in \left\{0,\ \tfrac13,\ \tfrac23,\ 1\right\},
\]
with every $k_i \ge 3$ mapping to $1$. On the deployment corpus $A^{\text{scene}}$ takes only $\{0.5, 1\}$ and $A^{\text{vlm}}$ only $\{0,1\}$. Three of the four inputs are therefore coarse by construction, and this is what bounds the resolution of everything built on them.

\section{Level 1: the Agreement term, and what it reduces to}

A fifth quantity is \emph{derived} from two of the four rather than measured:
\begin{equation}
A_i^{\text{agree}} = 1 - \left|A_i^{\text{object}} - A_i^{\text{vlm}}\right|
\label{eq:d-agree}
\end{equation}

Because $A^{\text{vlm}}$ is binary, Equation~\ref{eq:d-agree} has only two branches:
\begin{equation}
A^{\text{agree}} =
\begin{cases}
1 - A^{\text{object}} & \text{if } A^{\text{vlm}} = 0,\\[2pt]
A^{\text{object}}     & \text{if } A^{\text{vlm}} = 1.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:d-agree-cases}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Only half of the cases occur.} Because $A^{\text{vlm}} = \mathbb{I}[A^{\text{object}} > 0]$ on all $12{,}110$ images without exception (Section~\ref{sec:signal_decomposition}; both signals were read from the same VQA object count, Section~\ref{sec:canonical_saa}), only four of the eight combinations of object and VLM level occur. Substituting into Equation~\ref{eq:d-agree-cases}:
\begin{equation}
A^{\text{agree}} =
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{if } A^{\text{object}} = 0,\\[2pt]
A^{\text{object}} & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\label{eq:d-agree-reduced}
\end{equation}
Table~\ref{tab:agree_reduction} lists the values this leaves.

\section{Level 3: why \texorpdfstring{$U_{\text{SAA}}$}{U(SAA)} takes exactly eight values}

The uncertainty proxy is the sample standard deviation over the four terms:
\begin{equation}
U_{\text{SAA},i} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(a_{ij}-\bar{a}_i\right)^2},
\qquad
\mathbf{a}_i = \left(A_i^{\text{object}},\, A_i^{\text{agree}},\, A_i^{\text{scene}},\, A_i^{\text{vlm}}\right),
\ n=4
\label{eq:d-usaa}
\end{equation}
with $n-1=3$ in the denominator (\texttt{ddof}$=1$).

Counting the free inputs: $A^{\text{vlm}}$ is a function of $A^{\text{object}}$, and $A^{\text{agree}}$ is a function of both, so $\mathbf{a}_i$ is determined by the pair $(A^{\text{object}}, A^{\text{scene}})$ alone. With $4$ reachable object levels and $2$ scene levels,
\[
4 \times 2 = 8 \ \text{distinct vectors} \;\Longrightarrow\; 8 \ \text{distinct values of } U_{\text{SAA}}.
\]
A quantity presented as the spread of a four-model ensemble is therefore, in deployment, an eight-valued function of a saturating count and a binary flag (Table~\ref{tab:state_space}).

\section{A worked example}

The unanimous-present case is trivial: $\mathbf{a}=(1,1,1,1)$ gives $U_{\text{SAA}}=0$, $SAA=1$ and immediate auto-approval. The unanimous-\emph{absent} case is the one that carries the argument. Image \texttt{PPN1756555435/00000033\_2}, the second-highest-uncertainty state ($1{,}208$ images). Here $A^{\text{object}}=0$, so by Equation~\ref{eq:d-agree-reduced} the derived term jumps to $1$:
\begin{align*}
\mathbf{a} &= (0,\ 1,\ 0.5,\ 0), \qquad \bar{a} = \tfrac{0+1+0.5+0}{4} = 0.375\\
(a_j - \bar a) &= (-0.375,\ +0.625,\ +0.125,\ -0.375)\\
\textstyle\sum (a_j-\bar a)^2 &= 0.140625 + 0.390625 + 0.015625 + 0.140625 = 0.6875\\
U_{\text{SAA}} &= \sqrt{0.6875/3} = \sqrt{0.229167} = \mathbf{0.4787}\\
SAA &= 0.6(0) + 0.2(0.5) + 0.2(0) = \mathbf{0.1000}\\
\bar{c} &= 0.375 \quad\Rightarrow\quad U_{\text{triage}} = 0.6(0.4787) + 0.4(0.625) = \mathbf{0.5372}
\end{align*}
Escalated to a human. The single largest contribution to that spread, $0.390625$ of $0.6875$, comes from the \emph{derived} Agreement term rather than from any measured disagreement: the three measured signals here are $(0, 0.5, 0)$, which are close to one another.

\section{Levels 2 and 4}

The classification fusion and the deployed triage score are, for completeness:
\begin{align}
SAA_i &= 0.6\,A_i^{\text{object}} + 0.2\,A_i^{\text{scene}} + 0.2\,A_i^{\text{vlm}} \label{eq:d-saa}\\
U_{\text{triage},i} &= 0.6\,U_{\text{SAA},i} + 0.4\left(1 - \bar{c}_i\right), \qquad \bar{c}_i = \tfrac14\textstyle\sum_j a_{ij} \label{eq:d-triage}
\end{align}
Equation~\ref{eq:d-saa} omits both $A^{\text{ocr}}$ and $A^{\text{agree}}$; Equation~\ref{eq:d-usaa} includes $A^{\text{agree}}$ and omits $A^{\text{ocr}}$. The two are therefore not rescalings of one another, and in particular $U_{\text{SAA}} \neq 1 - SAA$. The error label of the corrected held-out evaluation thresholds Equation~\ref{eq:d-saa} at $0.5$ against the human label (the in-sample and conformal results use the stored four-term column; Caution~3 in Section~\ref{app:deployed_cautions}), which is the circularity quantified in Section~\ref{sec:signal_rebuild}.


\chapter{The Deployed Signals: Audit and Repair Sequence}
\label{app:audit_sequence}

Section~\ref{sec:canonical_saa} summarises the audit of the deployed Tier~1 signals. The audit confirmed the stored signals by re-running the fusion stage over the whole corpus, ran every Tier~1 model, including the trained detector, over all $12{,}110$ pictures, recomputed the experiments that consume the signals, and corrected each defect it found before measuring again. This appendix states what that sequence established and how it shaped the corrected design of Chapter~\ref{ch:corrected_design}.

\paragraph{What the sequence of repairs establishes.} Eight distinct defects have now been
identified and separately corrected: the derived Agreement term, the object-signal fallback, the
near-constant VLM term, the volume-based OCR term, the eight-valued discretisation, the circular
error label, the softmax scene channel, and the unanimous ensemble. Each was a real defect, and
each correction was carried through to a re-measurement. In no case does the corrected system
show disagreement outperforming a plain confidence baseline, and in the majority of cases the
correction \emph{widens} the gap. A negative result that survives eight separate repairs, each
of which had a plausible claim to being the cause, is considerably stronger evidence than a
single comparison would be; the repairs are therefore reported as the substance of
the finding rather than as failed attempts to avoid it.

\paragraph{What follows for the design.} The answer to ``what are these signals for'' is
different for each of the three, and only one of them survives as a trust signal.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Agreement should be removed, not repaired.} It cannot be made into a useful
  trust term, and its apparent usefulness in the deployed system is leakage: it scored $0.6835$
  by being a restatement of the one signal that does predict. Measured without that restatement it scores
  $0.5737$, and adding it to the spread costs $0.42$ ROC-AUC. Removing it also removes the
  asymmetry of Section~\ref{sec:signal_decomposition}, under which the estimator reported
  near-maximal uncertainty precisely where the ensemble was unanimously negative.
  \item \textbf{OCR should keep the repair but leave Tier~1.} $A^{\text{ocr}}_{\text{fix}}$ (the OCR signal discounted for degenerate repetition, Section~\ref{sec:signal_rebuild}) is a validity measure and belongs to the inscription reader's three-bucket assignment
  (Section~\ref{sec:agent5_ocr}), where validity is the question actually being asked and where
  the degenerate-repetition failure mode currently has no automatic detector. Its chance-level
  $0.4781$ against scene errors is not a failure of the repair: OCR validity and scene presence
  are unrelated quantities, and the deployed system was wrong to place it in a trust vector at all.
  \item \textbf{The VLM term should become Tier~2 content rather than a Tier~1 score.} The set of
  taxonomy classes the model names is informative --- it already drives the contradiction checker of Section~\ref{sec:agent2_contradiction} --- but compressed to a scalar it contributes $0.5569$ to trust, which is
  nothing. The set should be carried forward; the scalar should not.
\end{itemize}

This leaves Tier~1 trust resting on $A^{\text{object}}$ and $A^{\text{scene}}$, ranked by
confidence rather than by spread. That is not a reduction in capability: applied to the whole archive on the rebuilt signals at the same risk level,
confidence-based triage approves $61.1\%$ of the $12{,}110$ pictures automatically ($7{,}394$) against $9.9\%$ ($1{,}196$)
for spread-based triage, at higher error recall (Section~\ref{sec:formulation_search}). The $61.1\%$ is a share of the archive and is a different quantity from the $61.1\%$ approval of the deployed design in the labelled pool (Section~\ref{sec:corrected_results}). The three signals were not doing nothing --- two
of them were quietly re-encoding a third, and the resulting statistic was read as evidence of
cross-modal disagreement.

\section{Cautions on the Deployed Signals}
\label{app:deployed_cautions}
Levels 2 and 3 use \emph{different} signal sets: Level 2 omits Agreement and Level 3 includes it, while both omit OCR. Four cautions follow from the construction.

\paragraph{Caution 1: the Agreement signal is derived, not measured.}
Equation~\ref{eq:agree} makes $A^{\text{agree}}$ a function of $A^{\text{object}}$ and $A^{\text{vlm}}$, both of which also enter $U_{\text{SAA}}$, so the spread has four terms but at most three free inputs (two on the corpus; Caution~2). Section~\ref{sec:signal_decomposition} shows that this derived term carries the estimator's measured performance: removing it drops in-sample ROC-AUC from $0.9791$ to $0.7466$, and that a plain confidence baseline containing no disagreement information matches the full proxy in-sample and slightly beats it on the held-out partition.

\paragraph{Caution 2: the four terms of $U_{\text{SAA}}$ carry two independent inputs.} On all $12{,}110$ images $A_i^{\text{vlm}} = \mathbb{I}[A_i^{\text{object}} > 0]$ without exception, because both were read from the same VQA answer (Section~\ref{sec:canonical_saa}). $U_{\text{SAA}}$ is therefore a function of the pair $(A^{\text{object}}, A^{\text{scene}})$ and takes exactly \textbf{eight distinct values} over the corpus (Appendix~\ref{app:derivations}). This does not invalidate the triage built on it, since conformal risk control holds for whatever score it is given, but it explains why a plain confidence score is indistinguishable from it.

\paragraph{Caution 3: two error labels exist, and the stated one is not the deployed one.}
The three-agent $SAA_i$ defines the error label for the corrected held-out evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:clean_holdout}). A second, stored fusion column defines it for the in-sample $C_1$ result and for all Conformal Risk Control experiments; least squares recovers it exactly (maximum deviation $3.3\times10^{-7}$) as $\tfrac{1}{3} A^{\text{object}} + \tfrac{4}{15} A^{\text{agree}} + \tfrac{1}{5} A^{\text{scene}} + \tfrac{1}{5} A^{\text{vlm}}$, a four-agent fusion that contains the derived term. The two labels disagree on the scene decision for $22.1\%$ of the held-out pool. The conformal result does not depend on the choice: under both labels $\hat\lambda = 0.372$, test risk $= 0.0201$ and auto-approval $= 60.64\%$; only error recall ($91.07\%$ against $92.86\%$) and lift ($2.31\times$ against $2.36\times$) move. The in-sample discrimination figure moves more: $\text{ROC-AUC} = 0.9791$ under the deployed label but $0.9739$ under the three-agent definition stated above.

\paragraph{Caution 4: the standard deviation is the sample form.}
$\operatorname{std}$ at Level 3 is the sample standard deviation (ddof$=1$); the population form gives $0.174$ instead of $0.201$ on the corpus.

\section{Agreement-Weighted Label Filtering (AWLF)}
\label{sec:awlf}
The Agreement-Weighted Label Filter (AWLF) acts as a learned meta-classifier predicting pipeline errors. Despite its name it does not filter training labels; whether agreement between models can filter training labels is a separate question, tested in Section~\ref{sec:label_filter}. 

\paragraph{AWLF v1 (historical baseline pathologies).} In early iterations (AWLF v1), models were trained to predict binary \textit{scene presence}. However, the initial reference split contained extreme positive class imbalance ($319$ positive vs $2$ negative scans; $99.4\%$ positive rate), allowing trivial models to achieve an inflated $\text{F1}=0.9969$ simply by predicting positive for every instance. 

AWLF reads all six agent signals, including restoration quality and OCR, which $U_{\text{SAA}}$ excludes: a supervised classifier learns one coefficient per signal and need not treat them as commensurable, as an unweighted spread must.

\paragraph{AWLF v2 (error-targeted retraining).} To eliminate metric inflation, AWLF v2 redefines the target strictly as explicit classification failure:
\begin{equation}
    y_i = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } \mathbb{I}[S_{\text{fusion}, i} \geq 0.5] \neq y_{\text{expert}, i} \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
\end{equation}
In-sample, 5-fold cross-validated on $C_1$, the trained logistic meta-classifier achieves a mean $\text{ROC-AUC} = 0.9935$ across folds (the single fit on the full cohort scores $0.9938$; gradient boosting and random forest were also evaluated and scored $0.9905$ and $0.9908$; the linear model was retained for its interpretable per-agent coefficients, plotted in Figure~\ref{fig:awlf_v2_roc}). An independent estimate requires a partition that was never touched during weight, threshold, or classifier selection: on the original, contaminated group-level held-out test set (Section~\ref{sec:clean_holdout}, $n=401$), AWLF v2 appeared to achieve only $\text{ROC-AUC} = 0.6669$. Re-evaluated on the corrected $1{,}250$-image split ($n=249$ test, trained strictly on the disjoint train partition), AWLF v2 achieves $\text{ROC-AUC} = \mathbf{0.8702}$ -- well above the contaminated-pool estimate and consistent with Section~\ref{sec:clean_holdout}'s finding that the original apparent generalisation failure was a label-contamination artefact, not a limitation of the classifier, though below the in-sample figure as expected for a held-out estimate. Both figures share one caveat: the error label is defined by thresholding a fusion of the same agent scores the classifier reads, so part of the classifier's success is reading back the rule that made the label. Section~\ref{sec:signal_rebuild} measures that share for the disagreement signal at up to $0.26$ in ROC-AUC, and the same mechanism applies here.

On raw error count the deployed weighting looks the worst of the five ($70$ errors against $56$), with $5$ false positives where every alternative has $24$. That every alternative returns the \emph{identical} confusion matrix is the clue. Because $A^{\text{object}}$ takes four values, $A^{\text{scene}}$ two, and $A^{\text{vlm}} = \mathbb{I}[A^{\text{object}}>0]$, the fusion inputs realise just eight distinct cells. One of them, $(\tfrac13, 0.5, 1)$, holds $276$ of the $1{,}250$ images ($22.1\%$), and there the deployed weighting lands exactly on its threshold: $0.6(\tfrac13) + 0.2(0.5) + 0.2(1) = 0.5$. With $A^{\text{object}}$ stored as $0.333333$ the cell evaluates to $0.4999998$ and falls on the negative side; with exact thirds it is positive, and the deployed weighting gives $179/32/14/24$ with $56$ errors, the same as every alternative. The gap in Table~\ref{tab:weight_sensitivity} is one cell flipping because of a stored decimal, not because of the weights. The stored fusion column holds the exact $0.5$ and agrees with exact arithmetic on all $12{,}110$ images, so the canonical rule is exact arithmetic and the deployed row should be read as $56$ errors. Two error-label rules nevertheless appear in this appendix: the \emph{recomputed rule}, which recomputes the fusion from the truncated stored columns ($70$ errors on the test partition), and the \emph{stored-column rule}, which reads the stored fusion column ($56$); Table~\ref{tab:agree_ablation} reports both.

\paragraph{What moves with the rule.} Under the canonical rule conformal risk control is essentially unchanged ($\hat\lambda = 0.372$, auto-approval $60.64\%$, error recall $91.07\%$ against $92.86\%$, the bound satisfied at every $\alpha$). The blind-spot figure is not: on the $319$ confirmed negatives the canonical rule counts $204$ errors rather than $50$, and the disagreement AUROC moves from $0.1259$ to exactly $0.000$. On a confirmed negative an error \emph{is} ``$SAA$ above threshold'', so any score monotone in $SAA$ is perfectly anti-correlated with it there; the specific value $0.1259$ depends on the rule, and the deployment-framed comparison, where $U_{\text{SAA}}$ scores $0.7043$ against $0.7465$ for confidence, is the one to read (Section~\ref{sec:moved_negctrl}). A system whose behaviour on a fifth of its corpus rests on the seventh decimal of a stored constant is fragile in a way no accuracy figure shows.

\paragraph{A limit that no weighting reaches.} An image whose object signal is zero has two of the three fusion terms pinned to zero, so unless $w_s \ge 0.5$ it can never reach the $0.5$ threshold under \emph{any} weighting. On the corrected test partition this affects $32$ of the $211$ scene-positive images; on $C_1$ it affects $105$ of
$796$. No reweighting of the deployed signals can recover them.

\paragraph{Can the weights simply be learned?} Any weighting thresholded at $0.5$ induces one labelling of the eight cells, and linear programming shows that exactly \textbf{six} labellings are reachable under the deployed parameterisation (fifteen with a free threshold). Within them nothing improves on what is deployed: fitting by error count returns the degenerate all-positive rule $w=(0,1,0)$ (balanced accuracy $0.5$), fitting by balanced accuracy returns $w=(1,0,0)$ at $0.8136$ against the deployed $0.8113$ and reverses on test ($0.7720$ against $0.7802$), and constrained to the deployed false-positive budget the optimum \emph{is} the deployed classifier. For these signals the weights are not the part worth tuning (for the corrected design's scene classes they are; Section~\ref{sec:scene_classes}); the binding constraints are the discreteness of $A^{\text{object}}$ and its dependency with $A^{\text{vlm}}$.

\section{An Exhaustive Search Over Formulations}
\label{sec:formulation_search}

Section~\ref{sec:formulation_repair} shows that correcting the signal set lowers performance, and
Section~\ref{sec:signal_rebuild} shows the negative result survives a rebuild. Both invite the
same objection: that some better way of turning agent signals into an uncertainty score simply
had not been tried. This section closes that objection by searching the space systematically.

Nine formulations were evaluated against one held-out error label, defined by the rebuilt
ensemble's prediction against human review so that nothing is circular. They span three families:
\emph{which signals enter} (the deployed set, the derived term dropped, the redundant term also
dropped, all four re-derived from raw model outputs); \emph{how members are weighted}
(unweighted, weighted by competence measured on the training partition, and restricted to the
competent subset); and \emph{what operator is applied} (sample standard deviation, range, and the
mutual-information decomposition of Section~\ref{sec:signal_rebuild}). Table~\ref{tab:formulation_search} reports all nine.

\paragraph{What the search shows.} Removing the member that is at chance for scene presence makes the spread \emph{worse} ($0.7533$ to $0.6952$), weighting members by competence is worse again ($0.7000$), and keeping only the two strongest is worst. The failure is not the operator, the signal set or the weighting: a spread can only carry information when members are wrong about different things, which is a property of the ensemble, not of the formula.

\paragraph{A ceiling, not merely a survey.} Searching nine formulations leaves the objection that a
tenth might succeed. The eight-cell structure closes it. Any function of these signals is constant
within a cell, so its ranking of images is fixed by how it orders the eight; the best ordering
sorts cells by true error rate, and its ROC-AUC bounds every uncertainty score definable over
them. On the corrected held-out partition, against the deployed error label, that ceiling is $\mathbf{0.8871}$, confirmed by
evaluating all $8! = 40{,}320$ orderings rather than by argument. The bound is a property of
the \emph{deployed} signals, following from their eight-cell discretisation;
the full Tier~1 re-run of Section~\ref{sec:signal_rebuild}, whose rebuilt proxy takes $11{,}786$
distinct values, is not subject to it. What that re-run shows is that removing the
discretisation does not by itself put disagreement ahead of confidence either.

Against the same deployed error label ($70$ errors on the same $249$ pictures, not the non-circular label of Table~\ref{tab:formulation_search}), fused confidence ($1 - SAA$) reaches $0.8828$. It therefore sits within
$\mathbf{0.0043}$ ROC-AUC of the maximum any estimator over these signals could achieve. This is
the strongest form in which the negative result of Chapter~\ref{ch:uncertainty_calibration} can be stated: not that nine
formulations happened to fail, but that no formulation over the deployed signal set can beat a
plain confidence score by more than four thousandths of an ROC-AUC point, whatever its
functional form and however it is fitted. The remaining headroom is not a modelling problem, and
no better estimator can recover it.

\paragraph{Learning the combination.} Dropping $A^{\text{agree}}$ from the unweighted spread lowers discrimination ($0.8600$ to $0.8340$); dropping it \emph{and} learning the combination raises it to $0.8801$, but still not above confidence (paired difference $-0.0027$, $95\%$ CI $[-0.0160, 0.0109]$). Given both consensus and disagreement, a fitted risk model reaches $0.8828$, exactly what consensus alone reaches, because over eight cells the fitted combination reproduces the ordering consensus already gives.

\paragraph{Why the spread fails, measured.} The obvious explanation --- that the models are
wrong about the same images, so their spread cannot see the errors --- is not what the data show.
On the rebuilt signals the members' errors are essentially uncorrelated (pairwise $-0.107$ to
$+0.050$), all three are wrong together on only $6.2\%$ of images, and at least one is right on
$93.8\%$. This concerns the rebuilt signals of the deployed design and is a share of all images; in the corrected design the relevant share is of its errors, and $149$ of its $199$ errors are pictures on which all three agents are wrong (Section~\ref{sec:blind_spot_features}). Nearly uncorrelated errors are not the same as independent signals, however: as the next paragraph shows, these rebuilt signals span only $1.674$ independent directions, below the $2.0$ threshold of Section~\ref{sec:heterogeneity_precondition}, which only the deployed signals pass ($2.50$). These figures are computed on the softmax
scene channel, in keeping with the rest of this paragraph; on the corrected channel the
members agree slightly less often --- all three wrong together on $11.2\%$ and an effective
rank of $1.843$ rather than $1.674$ --- which moves the diagnosis in the direction of \emph{more}
heterogeneity, not less, while the spread still loses.

Three measurements locate the real cause. First, redundancy: the four signals have an effective
rank of $\mathbf{1.674}$ out of four, with $85.8\%$ of the covariance in a single direction, and
$A^{\text{agree}}$ correlates $-0.958$ with $A^{\text{object}}$ because it is built from it. A
spread over four signals that move along $1.7$ directions is close to a function of their level.
Second, and decisively, the spread does not separate errors: its mean is $0.4313$ on errors and
$0.4201$ on correct predictions, a difference of $0.011$, while the level differs by $0.289$
($SAA$ averages $0.335$ on errors against $0.624$ on correct ones), about twenty-six times larger.
Third, conditioning on the level removes what little remains: within quartiles of $SAA$ the
spread's ROC-AUC is $0.41$, $0.42$, $0.34$ and $0.74$ --- below chance in three of four strata.

The reason is structural rather than empirical. An ``error'' is defined as the fused score landing
on the wrong side of a threshold, which is a statement about the \emph{level} of the signals. A
standard deviation is a second moment, constructed to be insensitive to level. The proxy is
measuring the wrong moment of the right distribution, and no amount of signal quality repairs
that --- which is why giving the signals full resolution widened the gap instead of closing it.

the system re-scored, with every variant refitting its own threshold on calibration (Table~\ref{tab:what_can_go}).

\section{Can the Formulation Be Repaired?}
\label{sec:formulation_repair}

Three defects in the deployed definition have now been established: the Agreement term is
derived rather than measured (Section~\ref{sec:canonical_saa}); $A^{\text{vlm}}$ equals
$\mathbb{I}[A^{\text{object}}>0]$ on every image because both are read from one VQA answer; and
consequently $U_{\text{SAA}}$ is an eight-valued function of two free inputs. The natural
question is whether a corrected formulation recovers the reported performance.

The obvious repair --- delete the derived term --- does not work. Dropping $A^{\text{agree}}$
leaves $\operatorname{std}(A^{\text{object}}, A^{\text{scene}}, A^{\text{vlm}})$, which still has
only two free inputs and seven distinct values on the test partition, because the second defect
survives the first repair. Within the deployed table the only measured signal not tied to
$A^{\text{object}}$ is the OCR channel, and an independent four-signal vector requires
re-deriving everything from the raw model outputs.

\paragraph{What the monotonicity means.} Performance does not merely fail to improve as the
formulation is corrected; it falls at every step, from $0.860$ to $0.608$. The explanation is the
circularity of Section~\ref{sec:signal_decomposition}: the error label is defined by thresholding a
weighted mean of the deployed agents, so a signal built from those same agents predicts it well by
construction, and a signal built from independently re-derived quantities does not. The reported
figure was therefore substantially purchased by the degeneracy rather than achieved despite it.

The numbers in this thesis are those the deployed system produced and stand as its record; what changes is the interpretation: $0.860$ is the performance of a two-input score against a label those two inputs help define, not evidence that cross-modal disagreement detects errors.

\paragraph{What is unaffected.} The conformal guarantee bounds risk over whatever score it is
calibrated on, so it holds for every row of Table~\ref{tab:formulation_repair} (Section~\ref{sec:crc_distribution_free}). The pseudo-label collapse, the label-integrity correction,
the calibration result and the Tier~2 benchmarks do not depend on $U_{\text{SAA}}$ at all.

\section{The Review Queue at Archive Scale}
\label{sec:archive_queue}
\paragraph{Whether the labelled pool is representative, which is checkable.} Because the
signals realise only eight cells, the labelled pool's composition can be compared directly
against the corpus without collecting anything further. The total variation distance between
the two distributions is $0.076$. The pool over-represents the two lowest-signal cells --- the
$A^{\text{object}}=1/3$, $A^{\text{scene}}=0.5$ cell by $4.0$ percentage points and the
$A^{\text{object}}=0$ cell by $2.8$ --- and under-represents the confident
$A^{\text{object}}=1$ cell by $3.6$. In these signal cells the skew is mild. The comparison covers only the agents' signals, not the labels: Section~\ref{sec:archive_check} shows that under the pool's mixed labels only $16\%$ of the pool is ``no scene'', against $38\%$ of the archive ($39\%$ of the pool under one rule), and that a cut-off calibrated on the pool does not keep its promise on the archive. Closeness in the signals is therefore no evidence that the pool's error estimates carry over.

\paragraph{The review queue is one cell.} Escalation is not spread across the archive.
Of the $3{,}620$ images sent to a person, $2{,}217$ --- $61.2\%$ --- come from the single cell
$(A^{\text{object}}, A^{\text{scene}}, A^{\text{vlm}}) = (1/3,\,0.5,\,1)$, the same cell whose
classification Section~\ref{sec:weight_sensitivity} shows to hang on a stored decimal
truncation. A further $33.4\%$ come from the $A^{\text{object}}=0$, $A^{\text{scene}}=0.5$
cell. The human-review workload is therefore a statement about two cells rather than about
the archive, and any institution planning staffing from the $29.9\%$ figure should know that.

\paragraph{Why the threshold sits where it does.} $\hat\lambda = 0.372$ falls $0.00063$ below
that cell's triage score of $0.37263$. Moving the threshold by a single grid step, to $0.373$,
would move all $2{,}217$ images from review to unreviewed publication and lift auto-approval
from $70.1\%$ to $88.4\%$. That proximity looks alarming and is not: bootstrapping the $252$
calibration images $2{,}000$ times returns $\hat\lambda$ with a $95\%$ interval of
$[0.372, 0.372]$, and it crosses the cell in none of the draws. The threshold is pinned there
because crossing would auto-approve that cell's errors and breach the risk level --- the
conformal procedure is stopping exactly where its guarantee requires. The practical
consequence for a deploying institution is that review workload cannot be
reduced by tuning $\hat\lambda$. It is held in place by one cell, and only a different risk
level, or a signal that separates that cell, would move it.


\section{A Complementary Error Detector}
\label{sec:complementary_detector}
An error detector that uses evidence outside the decision is the natural complement to confidence. Built from raw signals that never enter $SAA$ (Grounding DINO counts and confidences plus OCR statistics for the strict set, since $A^{\text{object}}$ and $A^{\text{vlm}}$ both derive from the VQA answer) and cross-validated grouped by PPN volume, it reaches $0.7299$ (CI $[0.6536, 0.7994]$), or $0.7616$ (CI $[0.6827, 0.8338]$) with all raw signals. Both beat chance; neither separates from confidence (paired difference $+0.0148$, CI $[-0.0742, 0.0959]$). The item stands, now with a measured baseline to beat. A first version, trained inside the negatives-only pool, appeared to reach $0.7727$ and was discarded: there ``error'' and ``$SAA\ge0.5$'' are the same vector, so it was recovering the pipeline's own decision, and fitting both targets returned identical figures. That failure mode --- a target definitionally a function of the system under evaluation --- is the one that produced the original mistaken $\text{AUROC}=0.020$, and it recurs easily.

\section{The Risk-Control Algorithm as Deployed}
\label{sec:crc_algorithm_deployed}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:conformal_risk_control} is the conformal risk control procedure of Chapter~\ref{ch:triage_risk_control}, with the deployed triage score.

\begin{algorithm}[!htbp]
\caption{Distribution-Free Conformal Risk Control Calibration (CRC)}
\label{alg:conformal_risk_control}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Calibration split $C_{\text{cal}} = \{(\mathbf{I}_j, y_j)\}_{j=1}^{n_{\text{cal}}}$, Target upper risk bound $\alpha = 0.05$, Held-out test set $C_{\text{test}}$.
\Ensure Risk-controlling threshold $\hat{\lambda}$ satisfying $\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{R}(\hat{\lambda})] \le \alpha$ relative to the calibration distribution $C_{\text{cal}}$ (Sections~\ref{sec:risk_promise_explained} and~\ref{sec:archive_check} show how this guarantee behaves when new pictures differ from $C_{\text{cal}}$).
\State For each sample $j$: compute $\text{error}_j \leftarrow \mathbb{I}(\mathbb{I}[S_{\text{fusion}, j} \ge 0.5] \neq y_j)$ and $U_{\text{triage}, j} \leftarrow 0.6 \cdot \sigma_{\text{agents}, j} + 0.4 \cdot (1 - \bar{c}_j)$
\State For each threshold candidate $\lambda \in [0, 1]$ in steps of $0.001$:
\State \quad For each calibration sample $j \in C_{\text{cal}}$:
\State \qquad Compute non-conformity loss: $L_j(\lambda) \leftarrow \text{error}_j \cdot \mathbb{I}(U_{\text{triage}, j} < \lambda)$ \Comment{loss on auto-approved images only}
\State \quad Compute empirical calibration risk: $\hat{R}(\lambda) \leftarrow \frac{1}{n_{\text{cal}}} \sum_{j=1}^{n_{\text{cal}}} L_j(\lambda)$
\State Select the largest $\lambda$ satisfying the finite-sample bound (maximising auto-approval):
\State \quad $\hat{\lambda} \leftarrow \max \left\{ \lambda \in [0, 1] : \frac{n_{\text{cal}}}{n_{\text{cal}} + 1} \hat{R}(\lambda) + \frac{1}{n_{\text{cal}} + 1} \le \alpha \right\}$
\State \quad If no $\lambda$ satisfies the bound, set $\hat\lambda = 0$: nothing is approved and every picture goes to a person
\State Evaluate empirical test risk on $C_{\text{test}}$: $\mathcal{R}_{\text{test}}(\hat{\lambda}) \leftarrow \frac{1}{n_{\text{test}}} \sum_{k=1}^{n_{\text{test}}} L_k(\hat{\lambda})$
\State In-sample $C_1$ split: $\mathcal{R}_{\text{test}}(\hat{\lambda}) = 0.0075 \le \alpha = 0.05$ (bound satisfied). Group-level split on the corrected label pool (Section~\ref{sec:clean_holdout}): $\mathcal{R}_{\text{test}}(\hat{\lambda}) = 0.0201 \le \alpha = 0.05$ (bound \textbf{satisfied}) -- an original group-level split later found to be $66.7\%$ mislabelled on its negative-control class had shown $\mathcal{R}_{\text{test}}(\hat{\lambda}) = 0.182 > \alpha$; that failure did not survive the label correction.
\State \Return Threshold $\hat{\lambda}$: $0.5370$ from the in-sample $C_1$ calibration, $0.3720$ from the corrected group-level calibration at $\alpha=0.05$ (the deployed value); see Table~\ref{tab:crc_clean_vs_leaky}.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Further Detail for Chapters 2 to 5}
\label{sec:moved_detail}
The analyses below support Chapters~\ref{ch:methodology} to~\ref{ch:triage_risk_control}, which state their results and point here for the detail.

\subsection{A learned meta-classifier for the box-error target}
\label{sec:moved_meta}
To test whether combining signals helps on this target, a meta-classifier was trained on $C_2$ ($n=300$, $32$ positives, 5-fold stratified cross-validation) using the picture's human box count, cross-agent disagreement $\sigma$, mean agent confidence $\bar{c}$, the unified triage score $U_{\text{triage}}$, and the four Tier~1 agent scores. Logistic regression reaches $\text{ROC-AUC} = 0.622 \pm 0.097$, random forest $0.620 \pm 0.119$ and gradient boosting $0.604 \pm 0.093$ (mean $\pm$ standard deviation across folds), against $0.3965$ for the triage score alone. Two cautions limit what this shows. The fold-to-fold spread is of the same size as the gain over chance, and the box count is both an input and part of the label's definition, so the learner partly re-reads its own target. The result is a modest improvement over the triage score, not a resolution of the paradox.

\subsection{Why simpler diagnostics fail, and the derived term}
\label{sec:moved_precondition}
\paragraph{Why the obvious diagnostics fail.} The intuitive symptoms of a collapsed ensemble do not survive a control. On Europeana the VLM agent takes only two distinct values -- but it also takes only two across all $12{,}110$ Colibri images, where the deployed score's error ranking was measured. On Europeana the fusion score correlates $r=0.993$ with the object agent -- but on Colibri that same correlation is $r=0.980$, because the $0.6$ object weight makes $SAA$ object-dominated by construction on every corpus. The Europeana scene agent even takes \emph{more} distinct values than Colibri's ($22$ vs.\ $2$). Discreteness, and single-agent dominance of a weighted sum, are properties of this pipeline everywhere; neither indicates degeneracy.

\paragraph{The diagnostic does not depend on the derived term.} Because the Level-1 Agreement signal is algebraically determined by two of the others (Section~\ref{sec:signal_decomposition}), it is worth checking that the separation reported in Section~\ref{sec:heterogeneity_precondition} is not an artefact of including it. Computed over the three independent agents alone, the effective rank is $2.17$ ($95\%$ CI $[2.15,2.19]$) on Colibri against $1.50$ ($[1.06,1.79]$) on Europeana; the $2.0$ threshold separates the two regimes in both parameterisations, and the confidence intervals remain disjoint. The precondition of Section~\ref{sec:heterogeneity_precondition} therefore stands independently of the estimator issue.

\subsection{Relation to work on uncertainty under shift}
\label{sec:moved_shift}
Two further lines of work bear directly on the question. Ovadia et al.\ \cite{ovadia2019trust} compared uncertainty methods under increasing dataset shift and found that the calibration of every method degrades as the shift grows, ensembles least; the Colibri archive, scanned and restored historical prints scored by models trained on modern photographs, is such a shift. Jiang et al.\ \cite{jiang2022disagreement} showed that the rate at which two independently trained copies of a network disagree on unlabelled data approximates their test error, provided the copies are well calibrated, and Baek et al.\ \cite{baek2022agreement} found that across many models agreement in and out of distribution lies on a line, so that accuracy under shift can be estimated from agreement alone. Both results estimate the \emph{average} error of a population of models trained for the \emph{same} task and label space, and the first is proved only for calibrated members, while the second is an empirical regularity across many models of one task. The question here is different in two ways: the members are heterogeneous systems whose outputs must first be translated into one label space, and the target is to rank \emph{which picture} is wrong rather than to estimate how many are. Chapter~\ref{ch:corrected_tests} tests the per-picture question directly and finds the answer negative: the agents agree more than unrelated systems would (Section~\ref{sec:calibrated_agreement}), and in the pre-registered test and its follow-up within each task disagreement ranks errors no better than confidence, most errors being shared by all agents (Section~\ref{sec:c4_preregistered}). Section~\ref{sec:signal_decomposition} adds a structural reason that holds even where the agents' errors are nearly uncorrelated: a spread is insensitive to the level of the scores, while an error is decided by that level.

\subsection{Four attempts to overturn the decomposition}
\label{sec:moved_four}
Because the conclusion of Section~\ref{sec:signal_decomposition} is a negative one, it was probed further rather than left to stand on a single comparison. All four probes were fitted on the training partition of the corrected split and scored on its held-out test partition ($n=249$, $70$ errors). \emph{First}, the ensemble was given back the two \emph{measured} signals that no level uses -- generative restoration quality and grounded inscription -- since the comparison of Table~\ref{tab:signal_decomposition} may be unfair to the multi-agent construction in withholding them: it still does not beat a single agent ($0.8703$ vs.\ $0.8722$; paired $\Delta = -0.002$, $95\%$ CI $[-0.043, +0.038]$). \emph{Second}, a nested comparison asked whether $U_{\text{SAA}}$ adds anything \emph{on top of} confidence rather than instead of it: adding it to a confidence-only model moves ROC-AUC by $+0.001$ ($95\%$ CI $[-0.000, +0.004]$), so it does not. \emph{Third}, the shared-blind-spot failure of Section~\ref{sec:clean_holdout} turns out to be anticipatable even though it is not correctable: a gate trained to flag scene-absent images reaches ROC-AUC $=0.798$, but abstaining on the $10\%$ it flags most strongly improves error detection on the remainder by only $+0.013$ ($95\%$ CI $[-0.014, +0.040]$). \emph{Fourth}, removing agents one at a time -- an intervention on the pipeline, and so a stronger form of evidence than an attribution of credit -- shows the signal depends on the VLM agent ($0.860 \to 0.718$; $95\%$ CI $[-0.195, -0.098]$, excluding zero) and not at all on the object agent, whose removal changes ROC-AUC by exactly $0.000$. That zero is structural rather than numerical: removing the object agent maps the eight distinct $U_{\text{SAA}}$ values through a strictly increasing bijection (Spearman $=1.0$), so the ranking, and therefore any rank-based metric, cannot move. None of the four overturns the conclusion of Section~\ref{sec:signal_decomposition}, and the second and fourth sharpen it.

\subsection{Rebuilding the Signals and Retesting the Claim}
\label{sec:signal_rebuild}
The provenance correction of Section~\ref{sec:canonical_saa} gives the redundancy analysed in Section~\ref{sec:signal_decomposition} a mechanical cause rather than an emergent one: two of the four Tier 1 signals were read from a single VQA object count, so their perfect dependence is a property of the data path, not evidence about how the models behave. That makes the comparison in Table~\ref{tab:signal_decomposition} a weak test of the thesis's central claim, because the ensemble it evaluates was never four separately-measured quantities. The raw model outputs were retained for the full corpus, so the signals could be rebuilt and the claim retested.

Four quantities were re-derived for all $12{,}110$ images, each from a different model: mean detection confidence from Grounding DINO, scene probability from SigLIP re-run across the whole corpus, semantic agreement between the vision-language model's object list and the detector's labels, and OCR substance discounted for the degenerate repetition documented in Section~\ref{sec:agent5_ocr}. The resolution differs by orders of magnitude from the deployed vector: the rebuilt object signal takes $12{,}100$ distinct values against four, and the rebuilt scene signal $1{,}520$ against two, so the eight-valued ceiling on $U_{\text{SAA}}$ does not arise. Only $231$ images had SigLIP scores of their own before this run. Scored against the deployed error label on the held-out partition ($n=249$, $70$ errors), these rebuilt signals, which do not feed that label, reach a ROC-AUC of $0.6225$ as confidence and $0.6089$ as disagreement, against $0.8799$ for the deployed confidence that does feed it: most of the headline figure comes from the label sharing the score's inputs.

The retest removes the circularity identified in Section~\ref{sec:signal_decomposition} by holding one model out. One model predicts scene presence; the disagreement is measured over the remaining three only, so the predictor and the uncertainty signal share no input; and the error is defined against human review rather than by thresholding a fusion of the same agents. Rotating the held-out model gives independent replications. Two safeguards proved necessary. The labelled pool is about $84\%$ scene-positive, and an unweighted fit at a $0.5$ cut made three predictors constant, which silently made the error label identical to ``is a negative control'' and turned the procedure into a different experiment; predictors are therefore class-balanced with the decision threshold selected on the training partition, and a round is discarded if the predictor is constant or the label collapses. A round is also discarded if its predictor is at chance for scene presence, since the resulting error label carries no signal. The OCR round is excluded on that basis ($\text{ROC-AUC}=0.4974$).

On the held-out partition ($n=249$), disagreement does not outperform confidence in any of the three informative rounds. The paired differences are $-0.053$ ($95\%$ CI $[-0.213, +0.100]$), $-0.036$ ($[-0.166, +0.096]$) and $+0.022$ ($[-0.116, +0.160]$), every interval spanning zero. Asked instead whether disagreement adds anything on top of confidence, one round of three gives an interval excluding zero ($+0.089$, $[+0.009, +0.167]$) and the other two do not ($+0.079$ and $+0.076$, both spanning zero); no correction is applied across rounds, so an isolated positive of this size is weak evidence. The conclusion of Section~\ref{sec:signal_decomposition} therefore survives a test constructed to overturn it, on signals that are continuous and separately sourced and against a label the agents do not define.

\paragraph{Interpretation.} The original table's $241$ ``negative controls'' were $66.7\%$ mislabelled, so its $\text{AUROC}=0.020$ cannot stand. On verified labels the pipeline does well on the positive class ($0.9687$, close to the original $0.980$), and on the $38$ confirmed true negatives of the corrected test partition disagreement stays well below chance ($0.1152$), but on only $5$ errors ($95\%$ interval $[0.000, 0.396]$). A further $285$ images from the never-checked block, reviewed by the author and disjoint from every image used before, gave $123$ more true negatives; on them alone disagreement scores $0.1852$ ($95\%$ CI $[0.0799, 0.3059]$, $27$ errors), and on all $319$ confirmed negatives $0.1259$ ($50$ errors, $95\%$ CI $[0.0619, 0.1959]$). The direction is therefore replicated on independent data; the next paragraph shows that the value is largely arithmetic.

\paragraph{Why the value is below chance, and not merely low.} A below-chance AUROC invites the reading that the models are actively anti-correlated with error. Here the cause is arithmetic. On a true negative an error means $SAA \ge 0.5$; since $A^{\text{vlm}} = \mathbb{I}[A^{\text{object}}>0]$, an image with $A^{\text{object}}=0$ reaches at most $SAA=0.2$ and \emph{cannot} be an error. Of the $319$ confirmed negatives, $115$ sit in that stratum and carry $0$ errors. That is also where $A^{\text{agree}} = 1-|0-0| = 1$ makes the derived term a maximal outlier. The ranges separate cleanly --- $U_{\text{SAA}} \in [0.4787, 0.5774]$ on the $115$ guaranteed-correct images against $[0, 0.3849]$ on the $204$ carrying all $50$ errors --- so the proxy ranks every guaranteed-correct image above every error before any model output is consulted. Restricted to the stratum where errors are possible, the figure rises to $0.22$: about a quarter of the distance to chance is arithmetic, and the remainder is a residual failure. The exact value also depends on how one stored number is rounded: computed with exact thirds, the stratum's errors change and the figure falls to $0.000$ (Section~\ref{sec:weight_sensitivity}). The direction is robust; the value is not.

The subgroup is also circular. Because error is \emph{defined} as $SAA\ge0.5$ on a confirmed negative, the confidence baseline $1-SAA$ scores exactly $0.000$ here, by construction rather than by failure, and a subgroup whose natural baseline is $0$ cannot rank $SAA$-derived scores fairly. Posed as deployment poses it --- among the $968$ images the pipeline flagged scene-present with a human label ($918$ true, $50$ false, $409$ volumes, all human-sourced) --- the decision is constant and cannot leak: $U_{\text{SAA}}$ scores $0.7043$ and confidence $0.7465$. The limitation is therefore narrower than ``disagreement fails on negatives'': disagreement carries real above-chance information about which flagged images are wrong, and the below-chance figure belongs to a stratum degenerate for every score defined on $SAA$.

\subsection{The in-sample risk-control result}
\label{sec:moved_insample}
\textbf{Efficiency lift}, reported alongside every CRC result in this thesis, is defined as the concentration of errors the review queue catches relative to the corpus's overall error base rate:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Efficiency Lift} = \frac{\text{Error Recall}}{\text{Review (Audit) Fraction}} = \frac{\text{errors caught} / \text{images audited}}{\text{total errors} / \text{total images}}
\end{equation}
matching the deployed implementation exactly. All lift figures in this thesis are computed from the underlying unrounded rates (e.g.\ $88.53\%$ approval, $93.88\%$ recall, $11.47\%$ audit fraction $\to 8.18\times$ here) and only rounded for display; recomputing from the rounded display percentages (e.g.\ $93.9/11.5=8.17$) will not reproduce the reported figure exactly.

On the in-sample $C_1$ calibration/test split (the clean re-evaluation is in Section~\ref{sec:crc_distribution_free}), at $\alpha=0.05$ the test risk is $0.0075$, and the framework automatically approves \textbf{88.5\% of the collection} while capturing \textbf{93.9\% of true human classification errors} in the review queue ($8.18\times$ efficiency lift over random selection).
